# PART A — AGENTS ON THE CARD

## 1. ESMOLOL (IV)

**Card:** `500ug/kg load (over 1 min) + infusion 25-50ug/kg/min; then adjust by 25ug/kg/min q10-20min up to 300ug/kg/min | onset <1min | duration 10-20min | Ao dissection, CAD, peri-op HTN, avoid in ADHF`

**Class/mechanism.** Ultra-short-acting cardioselective (beta-1) beta-adrenergic antagonist. No vasodilator activity — BP falls only through negative inotropy and chronotropy. **[CCSAP]** is explicit: "Because of their β-selectivity, neither of these agents has direct vasodilatory effects, and blood pressure control is solely through the negative inotropic and chronotropic effects."

**Dosing [PI].** Loading dose "500 mcg per kg infused over one minute," then "50 mcg per kg per minute for the next 4 minutes." Titrate "at ≥ 4-minute intervals." Max for tachycardia 200 mcg/kg/min; for **hypertension**, "higher maintenance infusion dosages (250-300 mcg per kg per min) may be required" and "the safety of doses above 300 mcg per kg per minute has not been studied." Immediate perioperative control alternative: "1 mg per kg as a bolus dose over 30 seconds followed by an infusion of 150 mcg per kg per min." Maintenance infusions "may be continued for up to 48 hours."
**[CCSAP]** Table 1-5: "IV 25–300 mcg/kg/min (bolus of 500 mcg/kg not often required, given short onset); Titrate by 25 mcg/kg/min q3–5min."

**Onset.** **[CCSAP]** 1–2 min. **[IBCC]** 1–2 min. The label does not state an onset figure; it states steady-state beta-blockade is reached within ~5 min with a loading dose (30 min without one).
**Time to peak.** ~5 min at a given rate (steady state) with the load; ~30 min without.
**Duration after stopping [PI].** "Substantial recovery from beta blockade is observed in 10-20 minutes" post-infusion. Elimination half-life "approximately 9 minutes."

**Practical points.** Metabolized by "esterases in the cytosol of red blood cells" — organ-independent, so no dose change in hepatic or renal failure. **[PI]** warns of "severe reactions (thrombophlebitis, necrosis, and blistering), in particular when associated with extravasation. Avoid infusions into small veins or butterfly catheter." Large-volume drug at high weights.

**Cautions/contraindications [PI], verbatim:** "Severe sinus bradycardia," "Heart block greater than first degree," "Sick sinus syndrome," "Decompensated heart failure," "Cardiogenic shock," "Pulmonary hypertension," "Coadministration of IV cardiodepressant calcium-channel antagonists," "Known hypersensitivity to esmolol."

**Preferred where.** Aortic dissection (rate + dP/dt control), perioperative HTN, coexisting tachyarrhythmia, ACS. **[CCSAP]** Table 1-7 lists esmolol's indications as "Aortic dissection / Coronary ischemia/infarction" and notes it "Should be used in conjunction with an arterial vasodilator for BP management in aortic dissection."

**Monitoring.** Continuous BP/HR; ICU. No label mandate for an arterial line.

## 2. LABETALOL (IV)

**Card:** `10-20 mg load + 20-80mg bolus q10min OR 20 mg load + 0.5-2mg/min infusion, max dose 300 mg | onset <5min | duration 3-6h`

**Class/mechanism.** Combined alpha-1 and non-selective beta antagonist. **IV alpha:beta ratio is ~1:7** (oral ~1:3) — so given IV it behaves mostly as a beta-blocker **[CCSAP]**. **[IBCC]** makes the clinically important point: "The primary driver of most hypertensive emergencies is excessive *afterload (vasoconstriction)*. However, intravenous labetalol works predominantly as a beta-blocker."

**Dosing [PI], verbatim.** Repeated IV injection: "Administer 0.25 mg/kg up to 20 mg over 2 minutes. Administer 20 mg to 80 mg over 2 minutes at 10-minute intervals until a desired supine blood pressure is achieved." Cumulative max 300 mg — "the safety of doses above 300 mg has not been established."
Continuous infusion [PI]: **"Initiate at 2 mg/minute."** One cited study used a "mean dose of 136 mg…over a period of 2 to 3 hours."
**[CCSAP]** Table 1-5: "IV 0.5–10 mg/min; Titrate by 1–2 mg/min q2hr, given the agent's longer half-life, and consider dose reduction after BP control is achieved."

**Onset [PI].** "The maximum effect usually occurs within 5 minutes of each injection." **[CCSAP]** onset 2–5 min, peak 5–15 min.
**Duration.** **This is where the card is thin.** **[PI]:** after discontinuation, BP "rose gradually and progressively, approaching pretreatment baseline values within an average of 16 to 18 hours"; elimination half-life "approximately 5.5 hours." **[CCSAP]** Table 1-5 lists duration as "2–6 hr / **Up to 18 hr**."

**Practical points.** **[CCSAP]:** "though labetalol is often given by continuous infusion, its PK profile better supports intravenous bolus administration"; and "overaggressive, unintentional correction has been reported when labetalol is used in this manner. Because of the extended duration of action…each dose should be titrated cautiously." **[PI]** requires patients "remain supine and to proceed gradually in becoming ambulatory during and immediately following infusion (for up to 3 hours)."

**Cautions/contraindications [PI], verbatim:** "Bronchial asthma or obstructive airway disease," "Severe sinus bradycardia," "Heart block greater than first degree," "Cardiogenic shock," "IV administration of non-dihydropyridine calcium-channel antagonists," "Hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis, to labetalol." Also: "Avoid labetalol HCl injection in patients with overt congestive heart failure." Rare "severe hepatocellular injury…hepatic necrosis and death have been reported."

**Preferred where.** Stroke (ischemic and hemorrhagic), pregnancy/preeclampsia, aortic dissection (can be used as monotherapy — see #14), ACS. **[CCSAP]** Table 1-7 indications: "Acute ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke / Aortic dissection / Coronary ischemia/infarction / Pregnancy."

**Monitoring.** Continuous BP/HR. Can be given on a monitored floor as boluses; infusion generally ICU.

## 3. NITROPRUSSIDE (IV)

**Card:** `0.25-2ug/kg/min (dose limit to avoid cyanide toxicity), temporarily (<10min) can use up to max 10ug/kg/min | onset <1min | duration <2min | AS/LVSD and HF; avoid in ACS/CAD or CVA`

**Class/mechanism.** Direct nitric-oxide donor; balanced arterial + venous dilator (reduces both preload and afterload).

### BOXED WARNING — verbatim [PI]

> **WARNING: (A) EXCESSIVE HYPOTENSION; (B) CYANIDE TOXICITY**
>
> **(A) EXCESSIVE HYPOTENSION:** Sodium Nitroprusside can cause precipitous decreases in blood pressure which can lead to irreversible ischemic injuries or death. **Use only with continuous blood pressure monitoring** [see Dosage and Administration (2.2) and Warnings and Precautions (5.1)].
>
> **(B) CYANIDE TOXICITY:** Sodium nitroprusside metabolism produces dose-related cyanide, which can be lethal. A patient's ability to buffer cyanide will be exceeded in **less than one hour at the maximum dose rate (10 mcg/kg/min)**; limit infusions at the maximum rate to as short a duration as possible [see Warnings and Precautions (5.2)].

**Dosing [PI], verbatim.** "Continuously monitor blood pressure in patients receiving sodium nitroprusside. **Start infusion of sodium nitroprusside at a rate of 0.3 mcg/kg/min.** Evaluate blood pressure for at least 5 minutes before titrating to a higher or lower dose… **the maximum recommended infusion rate of 10 mcg/kg/min**." Renal dosing: "In patients with eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2, limit the mean infusion rate to less than 3 mcg/kg/min. In anuric patients, limit the mean infusion rate to 1 mcg/kg/min."
Must be given "by a volumetric infusion pump because small variations in infusion rate can lead to wide, undesirable variations in blood pressure." Do not mix with other drugs. Protect from light; discard if the solution turns blue, green or bright red.

**Onset [PI].** BP falls "within a minute or two after the start of an adequate infusion."
**Duration [PI].** "Dissipates almost as rapidly after an infusion is discontinued"; "circulatory half-life is about 2 minutes." Hypotension "should resolve within 1-10 minutes after discontinuation."

### Cyanide / thiocyanate — the full answer

**Where cyanide comes from.** Each nitroprusside molecule releases 5 cyanide ions. Cyanide is partly bound by methemoglobin; the remainder is converted to thiocyanate by hepatic rhodanese (transsulfuration, requires a thiosulfate sulfur donor), and thiocyanate is renally cleared.

**Dose/duration thresholds [PI]:**
- ">2 mcg/kg/min": "Sodium nitroprusside infusions above 2 mcg/kg/min generate cyanide ion (CN¯) faster than the body can normally dispose of it."
- At 10 mcg/kg/min: buffering capacity "exceeded in less than one hour."
- Buffering capacity: "A patient with a normal red-cell mass (35 mL/kg) and normal methemoglobin levels can buffer about 175 mcg/kg of CN¯, corresponding to a little less than **500 mcg/kg of infused sodium nitroprusside**." (That cumulative figure is the most useful bedside number — ~35 mg in a 70 kg adult.)
- **[CCSAP]** adds a practical threshold: toxicity is "associated with prolonged infusions (> 72 hr) or high doses (> 3 mcg/kg/min)."

**Who is at risk [PI]:** "Patients with hepatic dysfunction are more susceptible to cyanide toxicity." **[CCSAP]:** "Patients with chronic liver disease, alcoholism, and malnourishment may have a decreased capacity for transsulfuration, leading to an impaired ability to detoxify cyanide." **Renal failure drives thiocyanate accumulation, not cyanide** — [PI]: "Clearance of SCN¯ is primarily renal. In renal failure, the half-life can be doubled or tripled."

**Recognition [PI]:** "An early manifestation of cyanide toxicity is increasing dosage requirements to maintain blood pressure control." Also: "Cyanide toxicity causes venous hyperoxemia with bright red venous blood… Lactic acidosis may occur, but its emergence may lag other life-threatening manifestations… Acidosis may not appear until more than an hour after the appearance of dangerous cyanide levels. **Suspicion of cyanide toxicity is adequate grounds for initiation of treatment.**"

**Thiocyanate [PI], verbatim:** "Thiocyanate is mildly neurotoxic (tinnitus, miosis, hyperreflexia) at serum levels of 1 mmol/L (60 mg/L). Thiocyanate is life-threatening when levels reach ~200 mg/L. Therefore, **routine monitoring of plasma thiocyanate levels is recommended in patients with normal renal function when cumulative sodium nitroprusside doses exceed 7 mg/kg/day.** … Renal hemodialysis may be used to eliminate thiocyanate in cases of severe toxicity."

**Treatment [PI]:** stop the drug; "administration of sodium nitrite to convert as much hemoglobin into methemoglobin as the patient can safely tolerate; and then infusing sodium thiosulfate to convert the cyanide into thiocyanate. Hemodialysis is ineffective in removal of cyanide, but it will eliminate most thiocyanate." Sodium nitrite 3% solution, "4-6 mg/kg (about 0.2 mL/kg)…injected over 2-4 minutes"; sodium thiosulfate "150-200 mg/kg; a typical adult dose is 50 mL of the 25% solution." May repeat at half doses after 2 hours.
**[CCSAP]** reflects current practice better: "treatment with either intravenous hydroxocobalamin and sodium thiosulfate or sodium nitrite and sodium thiosulfate." **Hydroxocobalamin is the practical first-line antidote in the US and is not in the nitroprusside label.**

**Other label hazards:** methemoglobinemia (suspect after >10 mg/kg total); **increased intracranial pressure** — "Like other vasodilators, sodium nitroprusside can cause increases in intracranial pressure."
**Coronary steal** — **[CCSAP]** Table 1-7 lists "May result in coronary steal" and "Increases ICP," and gives its indication as "Most indications (excluding ICP elevations and coronary infarction/ischemia)." This supports the card's "avoid in ACS/CAD or CVA."

**Contraindications [PI], verbatim:** "Diseases with compensatory hypertension (e.g., coarctation of the aorta, arteriovenous shunting)"; "Inadequate cerebral circulation or in moribund patients (A.S.A. Class 5E) coming to emergency surgery"; "Patients with congenital (Leber's) optic atrophy or with tobacco amblyopia"; "Acute heart failure associated with reduced peripheral vascular resistance"; "Concomitant use with sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenifil, or riociguat."

**Outcome signal.** **[CCSAP]** Table 1-6 cites a retrospective ICH database analysis (n=1426): "Use of sodium nitroprusside associated with significantly higher multivariate-adjusted mortality (OR 1.6 [1.2–2.1]; p=0.001)" vs nicardipine.

**Monitoring.** Arterial line effectively mandatory — the boxed warning says "Use only with continuous blood pressure monitoring," plus a volumetric pump.

## 4. NITROGLYCERIN (IV)

**Card:** `Start 10-30ug/min, titrate by 10-20ug/min q5-10min; max 400 ug/min (if no response by 200 ug/min = non-responder) | onset 2-5min | duration 5-10min | ACS, flash pulm edema`

**Class/mechanism.** Organic nitrate / NO donor. **Predominantly a venodilator** (preload reduction) with dose-dependent arterial effect at higher rates. **[CCSAP]:** "nitroglycerin is primarily a venous vasodilator, yet dose-dependent afterload reduction is attainable." Coronary vasodilation "occurs without the complication of coronary steal" — the reason it beats nitroprusside in ACS.

**Dosing [PI], verbatim.** "Using nonadsorptive tubing, the initial adult dosage of Nitroglycerin in 5% Dextrose Injection should be **5 mcg/min**. … Initial titration should be in **5 mcg/min increments at intervals of 3 to 5 minutes. If no response is seen at 20 mcg/min, increments of 10 and even 20 mcg/min can be used.** … Once some hemodynamic response is observed, dosage increments should be smaller and less frequent." The label states **no fixed maximum dose**.
**[CCSAP]** Table 1-5: "IV 5–200 mcg/min; Titrate by 5–25 mcg/min q5–10min."
**[IBCC]** for ACS/angina: start 10–20 mcg/min, typical effect 20–50 mcg/min. For **SCAPE**: bolus 1,000–2,000 mcg over 2 min or 400–800 mcg/min for 2.5 min, then 100–300 mcg/min, up-titrating to 800 mcg/min if needed.

**Onset.** 2–5 min **[CCSAP]**. **Time to peak** a few minutes at a given rate.
**Duration after stopping.** 5–10 min **[CCSAP]**; serum half-life "about 3 minutes" **[PI]**.

**Tachyphylaxis — timing.** **[CCSAP]:** "One key consideration with the clinical use of nitroglycerin is the tachyphylaxis that occurs, possibly because of sulfhydryl depletion (because of the lack of a nitrate-free interval), requiring frequent escalations in dosing to maintain hemodynamic effects… **This tachyphylaxis usually occurs over the first 24–48 hours**, and if intravenous blood pressure control is still required at those time intervals, additional or alternative agents may be warranted." **[PI]** is blunter: "In the large majority of these trials, active agents were indistinguishable from placebo **after 24 hours (or less) of continuous therapy**." **[IBCC]** says tolerance develops within 12–24 hours. **Restoring efficacy needs a nitrate-free window** — [PI]: "drug-free intervals of 10-12 hours are known to be sufficient."

**PDE5-inhibitor contraindication interval.** [PI] contraindicates concomitant sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil and riociguat. Standard washout intervals before giving a nitrate: **sildenafil and vardenafil 24 h; tadalafil 48 h; avanafil 12 h.** (Consensus interval, e.g. Kloner et al., *Circulation* 2004;110:3149 — https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/01.cir.0000146906.42375.d3.) If a patient on a PDE5i needs BP/ischemia control, use a non-nitrate (CCB or beta-blocker) instead.

**RV infarction caution.** Not named in the nitroglycerin label, but the label does contraindicate use where "cardiac output depends on venous return (pericardial tamponade, restrictive cardiomyopathy, constrictive pericarditis)" — the same physiology. The RV-infarct caution is a guideline construct: the 2013 ACC/AHA STEMI and 2014 NSTEMI guidelines list RV infarction as a contraindication to nitrates because an infarcted RV is preload-dependent and venodilation drops cardiac output precipitously. **Honest caveat worth knowing:** the original evidence is a single 1989 observational series of 40 patients, and recent reviews question how absolute the prohibition should be (https://www.acepnow.com/article/revisiting-nitroglycerine-in-right-side-ventricular-involvement/). Practical rule stands: get a right-sided ECG (V3R–V4R) in inferior STEMI before nitrates.

**Other practical points.** **PVC tubing adsorbs nitroglycerin** — [PI]: "The delivered fraction of the solution's original nitroglycerin content has been 20-60% in published studies using PVC tubing." Use non-adsorptive/glass sets. Contraindicated in increased intracranial pressure. Dose-limiting headache/flushing is common **[CCSAP]**.

**Monitoring [PI], verbatim:** "Continuous monitoring of blood pressure and heart rate is necessary in all patients receiving this medication; in many cases, invasive monitoring of pulmonary capillary wedge pressure will also be indicated." Deliver "via an infusion pump that can maintain a constant infusion rate."

## 5. NICARDIPINE (IV)

**Card:** `Start at 5mg/h; inc by 2.5mg/h q5-15min; max 15mg/h | onset 5-10min | duration 15min-4h | SAH, CVA, Ao diss, FPE`

**Class/mechanism.** Dihydropyridine L-type CCB; peripherally selective arterial vasodilator with cerebral, coronary, renal and peripheral activity. Essentially no negative inotropy at therapeutic doses; does not raise ICP (unlike nitroprusside), which is why it is the workhorse in neuro ICU.

**Dosing [PI], verbatim.** "In a patient not receiving oral nicardipine, initiate therapy at **5 mg/hr** and **increase the infusion rate by 2.5 mg/hr every 5 minutes (for rapid titration) to 15 minutes (for gradual titration) up to a maximum of 15 mg/hr** until desired blood pressure reduction is achieved." After goal is reached by rapid titration, **decrease to 3 mg/hr**. "If unacceptable hypotension or tachycardia occurs, discontinue the infusion. When blood pressure and heart rate stabilize, restart the infusion at low doses such as 3-5 mg/hr."

**Onset.** 5–10 min **[CCSAP]**; [PI]: "With constant infusion, blood pressure begins to fall within minutes."
**Time to peak [PI], verbatim:** "It reaches about **50% of its ultimate decrease in about 45 minutes**." Full steady state at 24–48 h.
**Duration after stopping [PI]:** "Discontinuation of infusion is followed by a **50% offset of action in about 30 minutes**." **[CCSAP]** Table 1-5 duration: **2–6 hr**. **[IBCC]:** ~4–6 h.

**Why duration lengthens after prolonged infusion.** [PI] PK: nicardipine declines tri-exponentially — "a rapid early distribution phase (α-half-life of 2.7 minutes), an intermediate phase (β-half-life of 44.8 minutes), and a slow terminal phase (**γ-half-life of 14.4 hours**)." Short infusions are terminated by redistribution (alpha/beta phases). A long infusion loads the deep peripheral compartment, so offset is then governed by the 14.4-hour terminal phase. [CCSAP] footnote: "In patients for whom the agent is rapidly titrated, consider dose reduction after response is achieved, given the agent's long half-life." Practical consequence: after a multi-hour drip, expect BP to stay down for hours after you stop — do not chase it with more agents.

**Peripheral vs central line / phlebitis [PI], verbatim.** "To reduce the possibility of venous thrombosis, phlebitis, local irritation, swelling, extravasation…administer drug through **large peripheral veins or central veins**." And: "**To minimize the risk of peripheral venous irritation, change the site of the drug infusion every 12 hours.**" So: peripheral is acceptable (a large vein), but the site must be rotated q12h; central is preferred for long runs.

**Volume load.** A real ICU issue — premixed nicardipine is 0.1 or 0.2 mg/mL, so 15 mg/h at 0.1 mg/mL is 150 mL/h. **[IBCC]:** "Nicardipine is often provided in a relatively *dilute* form that may cause substantial volume administration." Use the 0.2 mg/mL bag in volume-sensitive patients.

**Cautions/contraindications [PI]:** "Do not use in patients with advanced aortic stenosis" (afterload reduction without contractile reserve). "Titrate slowly…particularly in combination with a beta-blocker, in patients with heart failure." "Consider lower dosages and closely monitor responses in patients with impaired liver function" (CYP3A4/2C8/2D6 metabolism — **[CCSAP]** notes prolonged effects in chronic liver disease). "Titrate gradually in patients with renal impairment." Reflex tachycardia **[CCSAP]**. Do not co-infuse in the same line: "Do not combine Cardene I.V. Premixed Injection with any product in the same intravenous line or premixed container."

**Preferred where.** ICH, SAH, ischemic stroke (best BP-variability profile), aortic dissection (after beta-blockade), flash pulmonary edema, post-op. **[CCSAP]** comparative data are strongly in its favour vs labetalol: greater goal attainment (83% vs 67%, p=0.04), fewer add-on agents (17% vs 31%, p=0.02), fewer hypotensive events (10% vs 19%, p=0.04) in a mixed ICU population; in stroke, fewer dose adjustments (2 vs 4, p<0.001) and less BP variability.

**Monitoring [PI]:** "Monitor blood pressure and heart rate continually during infusion and avoid too rapid or excessive blood pressure drop." Arterial line preferred but not label-mandated.

## 6. CLEVIDIPINE (IV)

**Card:** `Start at 1-2mg/h; most respond to 4-6mg/h, max 21mg/h | onset 2-4min | duration 5-15min | SAH, CVA, Ao diss, FPE`

**Class/mechanism.** Third-generation dihydropyridine CCB in a soy-oil-in-water lipid emulsion. Arterial-selective; no venodilation, no effect on filling pressures. Metabolized by **blood and tissue esterases** (organ-independent) — the reason it is the most titratable agent available.

**Dosing [PI], verbatim.** "Initiate the intravenous infusion of Cleviprex at **1-2 mg/hour**. … The dose may be doubled at short (**90 second**) intervals initially. As the blood pressure approaches goal, the increase in doses should be less than doubling and the time between dose adjustments should be lengthened to **every 5-10 minutes**. … The desired therapeutic response for most patients occurs at doses of **4-6 mg/hour**. … Most patients were treated with maximum doses of **16 mg/hour** or less. There is limited short-term experience with doses up to **32 mg/hour**."

**The 21 mg/h number — read it correctly [PI]:** "**no more than 1000 mL or an average of 21 mg/hour of Cleviprex infusion is recommended per 24 hour period.**" This is a **24-hour average cap driven by the lipid volume**, not an instantaneous ceiling. The instantaneous ceiling is 16 mg/h (usual max studied), with limited data to 32 mg/h.

**Lipid load.** "Cleviprex contains approximately **0.2 g of lipid per mL (2.0 kcal)**." Volume limit **1000 mL/24 h** (= 20 × 50 mL vials or 10 × 100 mL vials). **[CCSAP]:** "Because of the lipid load associated with infusion, it is recommended to give less than 1000 mL of clevidipine per 24-hour period (average of 21 mg/hour) with consideration of **triglyceride monitoring and coadministration of other lipid emulsions**." Count propofol and parenteral nutrition against the same lipid budget. **[IBCC]** suggests triglycerides q24–48 h. Post-marketing adverse reaction: "increased blood triglycerides."

**Contraindications [PI], verbatim (all three):**
1. "**Allergy to soy or eggs**" (soybean oil + egg yolk phospholipid emulsifier)
2. "**Defective lipid metabolism**" — e.g. pathologic hyperlipidemia, lipoid nephrosis, acute pancreatitis with hyperlipidemia
3. "**Severe aortic stenosis**"

**Onset [PI].** "Cleviprex produces a 4-5% reduction in systolic blood pressure **within 2-4 minutes**."
**Time to peak.** Within minutes at a given rate; initial-phase half-life ~1 min.
**Duration after stopping [PI].** "In most patients, full recovery of blood pressure is achieved in **5-15 minutes** after the infusion is stopped." Terminal half-life ~15 min. Effect does **not** accumulate with prolonged infusion (contrast nicardipine).

**Other practical points.**
- **Rebound hypertension [PI]:** "Patients who receive prolonged Cleviprex infusions and are not transitioned to other antihypertensive therapies should be monitored for the possibility of rebound hypertension for **at least 8 hours** after the infusion is stopped."
- **Vial handling [PI]:** "Once the stopper is punctured, use within **12 hours** and discard any unused portion" — lipid emulsions support bacterial growth **[CCSAP]**. Strict aseptic technique. Store refrigerated 2–8°C.
- Reflex tachycardia **[CCSAP]**.
- Much lower volume than nicardipine at equivalent effect — the main advantage in volume-overloaded patients.

**Cost relative to nicardipine.** **[CCSAP]:** "nicardipine is about **one-third the cost of clevidipine**, which is about one-fourth the cost of sodium nitroprusside per vial." A 2024 AJHP ICU comparison reported clevidipine at **$199.37 per vial (AWP, June 2023), 682% higher than a bag of nicardipine** (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38923443/). Practical read: clevidipine is a several-fold cost step up from nicardipine for no demonstrated hard-outcome benefit — reserve it for volume-sensitive patients and those needing the fastest offset.

**Monitoring [PI]:** "Monitor blood pressure and heart rate continually during infusion, and then until vital signs are stable."

## 7. ENALAPRILAT (IV) — the only IV ACE inhibitor

**Class/mechanism.** ACE inhibitor; the active de-esterified metabolite of enalapril. Blocks angiotensin I → II conversion, lowering vasoconstriction and aldosterone.

**Dosing [PI], verbatim.** "**1.25 mg every six hours administered intravenously over a five minute period.**" On a diuretic: "0.625 mg administered intravenously over a five minute period," may repeat after one hour, then 1.25 mg q6h. Renal: CrCl >30 mL/min → standard; **CrCl ≤30 mL/min → 0.625 mg** initial, may repeat after one hour. Conversion: 1.25 mg IV q6h → enalapril 5 mg PO daily; 0.625 mg IV q6h → 2.5 mg PO daily.
**[CCSAP]** Table 1-5: "IV bolus: 1.25 mg q6hr; Titrate no more than q12–24hr…; max dose: 5 mg q6hr."

**Onset [PI].** "A clinical response is usually seen **within 15 minutes**."
**Time to peak [PI].** "Peak effects may not occur for up to **four hours** after dosing."
**Duration.** [PI]: "approximately **six hours**" at the recommended dose, and "the duration of hemodynamic effects appears to be **dose-related**." **[CCSAP]** Table 1-5 lists **12–24 hr** and states plainly: "**A bolus dose may last 12–24 hours, making dose adjustment difficult and raising significant safety concerns.**"

**Why it is a poor titration agent.** Three compounding problems: (1) 15-minute onset but a peak that can be **4 hours** away, so you cannot see the effect of a dose before the next decision point; (2) duration is **dose-related and long** (6 h label, 12–24 h in practice) with a titration interval of q12–24 h per [CCSAP] — there is no way to walk a dose back; (3) the magnitude of response depends on the patient's renin state (volume-depleted / high-renin patients drop precipitously; volume-expanded low-renin patients barely respond), so the dose–response is not predictable at the bedside.

**BOXED WARNING — fetal toxicity [PI], verbatim:** "When used in pregnancy during the second and third trimesters, ACE inhibitors can cause injury and even death to the developing fetus. When pregnancy is detected, enalaprilat injection, USP should be discontinued as soon as possible." **[CCSAP]:** "enalaprilat must be avoided in pregnant patients."

**Contraindications [PI], verbatim:** "Hypersensitive to any component of this product and in patients with a history of angioedema related to previous treatment with an angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitor and in patients with hereditary or idiopathic angioedema."

**Other cautions.** Hypotension in salt/volume depletion; hyperkalemia ("greater than 5.7 mEq/L…in approximately one percent"); rising BUN/creatinine (bilateral renal artery stenosis, hypoperfusion). **[CCSAP]:** "use may be associated with deterioration of renal function, especially in states of poor renal perfusion that potentially occur in hypertensive emergency, warranting avoidance or great caution with renal impairment." Angioedema risk.

**Where it is preferred.** **[CCSAP]** Table 1-7 indication: "Acute left ventricular failure." Niche: hypertensive acute LV failure where you want RAAS blockade and cannot use PO; scleroderma renal crisis is treated with **oral captopril**, not enalaprilat.

**Monitoring.** Continuous BP; ICU not mandatory since it is a q6h push.

**Card verdict: DOES NOT BELONG on the ICU drip table.** It is not titratable and is genuinely dangerous to use that way. A one-line mention under "avoid for titration" would be reasonable; an entry with an onset/duration column invites misuse. Absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy (boxed).

## 8. FENOLDOPAM — **NO LONGER MARKETED IN THE US**

**Availability — the headline.** **DailyMed returns zero labels for "fenoldopam" as of 2026-09-21.** Pfizer/Hospira has discontinued all Corlopam presentations (US discontinuation dated 2023); AHFS states the monograph is no longer updated because the drug is off the US market. https://www.drugs.com/availability/generic-corlopam.html ; https://www.drugs.com/monograph/fenoldopam.html

**Class/mechanism.** Selective peripheral **dopamine-1 (D1) receptor agonist** — the only one in this class. **[CCSAP]:** "a peripherally acting dopamine-1 (D1) receptor agonist with activity in the coronary, **renal (both afferent and efferent)**, mesenteric, and peripheral arteries." No alpha or beta activity.

**Dosing (Corlopam PI, https://www.pfizermedical.com/corlopam/highlights).** Adults: initial **0.01–0.3 mcg/kg/min** by continuous IV infusion; increase by **0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min every ≥15 minutes**. **No bolus.** Pediatric: start 0.2 mcg/kg/min, titrate 0.3–0.5 mcg/kg/min q20–30 min, max 0.8 mcg/kg/min.
**[CCSAP]** Table 1-5: "IV 0.03–1.6 mcg/kg/min; Titrate by 0.05–1 mcg/kg/min q15min."

**Onset.** 10–15 min **[CCSAP]**; steady-state plasma concentration in ~20 min.
**Time to peak.** ~15–20 min per dose step (hence the ≥15-min titration interval).
**Duration after stopping.** 10–15 min **[CCSAP]** (plasma half-time ~5 min), though the practical offset is 30–60 min in some series.

**The renal-perfusion claim — and whether the evidence supports it.** Fenoldopam's selling point was that D1 agonism dilates both afferent and efferent renal arterioles, increasing renal blood flow, natriuresis and diuresis — a "renal-protective" antihypertensive. **The evidence does not support a hard-outcome benefit.** The largest RCT (Bove et al., *JAMA* 2014, n=667, cardiac-surgery AKI) found that fenoldopam "did not reduce the need for renal replacement therapy or risk of 30-day mortality but **was associated with an increased rate of hypotension**" — the trial was stopped for futility. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25265449/ . Smaller prevention trials showed lower AKI incidence (e.g. Ranucci 2010: AKI 12.6% vs 27.6%) but no mortality/RRT benefit. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11477789/ . Net: surrogate renal endpoints improve, patient-important endpoints do not.

**Glaucoma — correct the common teaching.** The Corlopam PI lists **CONTRAINDICATIONS: None.** Increased intraocular pressure is a **WARNING**, not a contraindication: "Increased intraocular pressure in patients with glaucoma or intraocular hypertension." **[CCSAP]:** "Caution with increases in ICP or intraocular pressure… can worsen glaucoma." So the widely repeated "contraindicated in glaucoma" is an overstatement of a warning.

**Sulfite content.** Contains **sodium metabisulfite** — PI: "a sulfite that may cause allergic-type reactions." **[CCSAP]:** "fenoldopam contains sodium metabisulfite, which can trigger anaphylactic reactions in those with sulfa or sulfite allergies." Ampules contain 1 mg sodium metabisulfite per mL.

**Other cautions.** Dose-related **tachycardia** (especially >0.1 mcg/kg/min); **hypokalemia** — check K+ q6h; headache, flushing, nausea. Raises ICP.

**Card verdict: CORRECTLY OMITTED — leave it off.** It is not obtainable in the US. If it is retained anywhere it should be a footnote ("D1 agonist, discontinued in the US 2023"). Do not teach interns a drug they cannot order, and do not teach the renal-protection claim as established.

## 9. PHENTOLAMINE — the catecholamine-excess agent

**Class/mechanism.** Non-selective, competitive **alpha-1 and alpha-2** adrenergic antagonist producing direct arterial vasodilation. **[CCSAP]:** "a peripheral α1 and α2 receptor antagonist leading to direct vasodilation." The PI describes "an alpha-adrenergic block of relatively short duration."

**Dosing [PI], verbatim.** Pheochromocytoma, preoperative: "**5 mg** of Phentolamine Mesylate for Injection, USP (1 mg for children) is injected intravenously or intramuscularly **1 or 2 hours before surgery**." Intraoperative: "5 mg for adults, 1 mg for children) is administered intravenously as indicated."
**[CCSAP]** Table 1-5 for hypertensive emergency: "**IV bolus: 1–5 mg PRN; max 15 mg**."
**Norepinephrine/vasopressor extravasation [PI], verbatim:** "**5-10 mg** of Phentolamine Mesylate for Injection, USP in **10 mL of saline** is injected into the area of extravasation **within 12 hours**." (Infiltrate the area subcutaneously with a fine needle.)

**Onset.** Immediate — **[CCSAP]** Table 1-5 lists onset in "seconds."
**Time to peak.** ~2 min after IV bolus.
**Duration after stopping.** **[CCSAP]** Table 1-5: **~15 min**. [PI]: "Half-life in the blood of **19 minutes** following intravenous administration." Because of the short duration, repeated boluses or a continuous infusion (unlabeled, ~0.2–0.5 mg/min titrated) are used for sustained control.

**Indications where it is the agent of choice.**
- **Pheochromocytoma / paraganglioma** — labeled indication; also the crisis drug during tumor manipulation.
- **Cocaine, methamphetamine and other sympathomimetic toxicity** — **[CCSAP]** Table 1-7: "Use in catecholamine-induced hypertensive emergency. If used for cocaine-induced HTN crisis – Use in conjunction with BZDs." **Benzodiazepines are first-line**; phentolamine is for BP/coronary vasoconstriction refractory to benzodiazepines.
- **MAOI–tyramine ("cheese") reaction** — classic indication; the hypertension is pure catecholamine surge.
- **Clonidine (or other central alpha-2 agonist) withdrawal rebound** — driven by catecholamine surge; alpha blockade is rational. Note: **restarting clonidine is the definitive treatment**; a pure beta-blocker is the thing to avoid here.
- **Vasopressor extravasation** — the labeled dermal-necrosis indication above.

**Why beta-blockers are the wrong reflex in catecholamine excess.** Blocking beta-2-mediated vasodilation while alpha-1 is unopposed can worsen vasoconstriction and BP. The 2008 AHA statement on cocaine-associated chest pain recommends against beta-blockers; if one is used, a combined alpha/beta agent (labetalol, carvedilol) is preferred over a beta-selective agent. Discussion: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28399647/ . For pheochromocytoma the rule is firm: **alpha blockade before beta blockade.**

**Contraindications [PI], verbatim:** "Myocardial infarction, history of myocardial infarction, coronary insufficiency, angina or other evidence suggestive of coronary artery disease; hypersensitivity to phentolamine or related compounds."
**Warnings [PI], verbatim:** "Myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular spasm, and cerebrovascular occlusion have been reported." "Tachycardia and cardiac arrhythmias may occur." **[CCSAP]:** "rebound tachycardia can occur, which can lead to an oxygen supply-and-demand mismatch in patients with coronary artery disease, inducing angina or myocardial infarction." Flushing and headache are common.

**Monitoring.** Continuous BP; ICU for repeat dosing/infusion.

**Card verdict: SHOULD BE ADDED.** There is no substitute for it in the catecholamine-excess presentations, and the card currently offers no agent for pheo/cocaine/MAOI/clonidine-withdrawal crisis. One line is enough: *Phentolamine 5 mg IV q10–15 min PRN (1–5 mg boluses, max ~15 mg); onset seconds, duration ~15 min; catecholamine excess — pheo, cocaine/meth (after benzos), MAOI–tyramine, clonidine withdrawal, vasopressor extravasation 5–10 mg in 10 mL NS.*

## 10. METOPROLOL TARTRATE (IV)

**Label:** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=7c1f998a-233d-4feb-6ea2-457702abd0d2 (Hospira, rev. 5/2024; corroborated on the Fosun and Civica labels)

**Class/mechanism.** Beta-1 selective adrenergic antagonist. No alpha activity, therefore **no vasodilation**.

### The headline: hypertension is NOT an approved IV indication

[PI] §1, verbatim — and this is the **entire** indications section:
> "Metoprolol tartrate Injection is indicated in the treatment of definite or suspected acute myocardial infarction in hemodynamically stable patients to reduce cardiovascular mortality when used in conjunction with oral metoprolol maintenance therapy."

The hypertension indication exists only on the **oral** label ("Metoprolol tartrate is indicated for the treatment of hypertension in adult patients, to lower blood pressure" — https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=485f1fdb-6543-4f47-e063-6394a90a2459). **Every use of IV metoprolol to lower blood pressure is off-label.**

**Dosing [PI], verbatim** (the only IV regimen in the label — no infusion, no repeat schedule, no stated cumulative max):
> "Begin treatment in this early phase with the intravenous administration of three bolus injections of 5 mg of Metoprolol tartrate each; give the injections at approximately **2-minute intervals**. During the intravenous administration of Metoprolol tartrate, monitor blood pressure, heart rate, and electrocardiogram."
**Note the card-relevant detail: the label interval is q2 min, not q5 min.** The familiar "5 mg q5min × 3" is a practice modification. **[CCSAP]** Table 1-5 for hypertensive emergency gives "IV bolus: **5–15 mg q5–15min PRN**."

**Onset.** **The label states no onset of action.** It gives only time to *maximum* beta-blockade [PI] §12.2, verbatim: "When the drug was infused over a 10-minute period, in normal volunteers, maximum beta-blockade was achieved at approximately **20 minutes**." **[CCSAP]** Table 1-5 lists onset **5–20 min**.
**Duration.** **The label states no duration of effect.** **[CCSAP]** Table 1-5: **2–6 hr**.
**Half-life [PI], verbatim:** "the plasma half-life ranges from approximately **3 to 7 hours**."

### Why it is a rate agent more than a BP agent

Three independent reasons, all sourced:

1. **Mechanism.** Beta-1 selectivity means no vasodilation — BP falls only via reduced cardiac output. **[CCSAP]:** "Because of their β-selectivity, neither of these agents has direct vasodilatory effects, and blood pressure control is solely through the negative inotropic and chronotropic effects." In a hypertensive emergency driven by systemic vasoconstriction, that is the wrong lever.
2. **The label's own PD data do not support it as a BP drug.** [PI] §12.2, verbatim: "In several studies of patients with acute myocardial infarction, intravenous followed by oral administration of Metoprolol tartrate caused a reduction in heart rate, systolic blood pressure and cardiac output. **Stroke volume, diastolic blood pressure and pulmonary artery end diastolic pressure remained unchanged.**" Systolic falls; **diastolic does not move**.
3. **Kinetics make it untitratable.** **[CCSAP]**, verbatim: "**Metoprolol has β-selectivity similar to esmolol, but given its slower onset, intravenous push administration, and longer duration of activity, metoprolol has less titratability and can lead to extended, overaggressive, unintentional correction, placing patients at risk of induced ischemic complications… Intravenous metoprolol is thus often avoided for this indication.**"

### COMMIT — the safety caution behind early IV beta-blockade in ACS

COMMIT/CCS-2 (Lancet 2005;366:1622, n=45,852 acute MI) randomized early IV metoprolol (up to 15 mg) followed by high-dose oral metoprolol vs placebo. The regimen **did not reduce** the composite of death/reinfarction/cardiac arrest. It reduced reinfarction (OR 0.82, 95% CI 0.72–0.92) and ventricular fibrillation (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.75–0.93) but **increased cardiogenic shock (OR 1.30, 95% CI 1.19–1.41)**. The excess shock was concentrated in patients with higher Killip class / hemodynamic instability: "substantial net hazard in haemodynamically unstable patients, and moderate net benefit in those who were relatively stable." https://www.thelancet.com/article/S0140-6736(05)67661-1/abstract ; https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Clinical-Trials/2011/08/07/19/11/COMMITCCS2--Metoprolol
This is why the IV label restricts the indication to "**hemodynamically stable**" patients, and it is the evidentiary basis for the card's Table 2 line "No BB if LV failure w/ pulm edema, HR <60, SBP <100, shock, high-grade HB" — **that line is CONFIRMED and well-calibrated.**

**Contraindications [PI], verbatim (note the specific numeric thresholds):**
> "Hypersensitivity to Metoprolol tartrate and related derivatives, or to any of the excipients; hypersensitivity to other beta-blockers (cross sensitivity between beta-blockers can occur). Metoprolol tartrate is contraindicated in patients with a **heart rate <45 beats/min**; second- and third-degree heart block (unless a functioning pacemaker is present); significant first-degree heart block (**P-R interval ≥0.24 sec**); **systolic blood pressure <100 mmHg**; or decompensated cardiac failure."

**Warnings.** Bronchospasm [PI] §5.3, verbatim (the label's own capitals): "PATIENTS WITH BRONCHOSPASTIC DISEASES, IN GENERAL, SHOULD NOT RECEIVE BETA-BLOCKERS because they can exacerbate bronchospasm. Because of its relative beta1 cardio-selectivity, however, metoprolol may be used in patients with bronchospastic disease for initial treatment of myocardial infarction." Bradycardia/heart block/cardiac arrest have occurred. Worsening cardiac failure may occur.
**Abrupt cessation: the warning is NOT on the IV label** — it appears only on the oral label (taper over 1–2 weeks in ischemic heart disease). **No boxed warning** on the IV label.

**Preferred where.** Acute MI in a hemodynamically stable patient (labeled). Rate control of AF/AFL/sinus tachycardia (off-label but standard). Aortic dissection — **[CCSAP]** Table 1-7 lists it alongside esmolol with the same caveat: "Should be used in conjunction with an arterial vasodilator… (initiate metoprolol first because of its delayed onset relative to vasodilators)" and "Contraindicated in acute decompensated heart failure."

**Monitoring.** Label requires BP, HR and ECG monitoring during administration. No ICU or arterial line requirement.

**Card verdict: ADD ONE LINE TO THE FLOOR TABLE, explicitly flagged as a rate agent.** Do not put it in the ICU drip table. Suggested line: *Metoprolol tartrate IV 5 mg q2–5 min ×3; onset 5–20 min, duration 2–6 h. **Rate agent, not a BP agent** — no vasodilation, DBP unchanged, not FDA-approved IV for HTN; overcorrection is unpredictable.*

## 11. DILTIAZEM (IV)

**Label:** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=bba03666-ff4f-4bf6-fa92-78e1ec2d9073 (Hospira, rev. 5/2026)

**Class/mechanism.** Non-dihydropyridine (benzothiazepine) L-type CCB. Unlike the dihydropyridines it acts preferentially on the AV node and myocardium as well as the vasculature. **[CCSAP]:** "the non-dihydropyridine agents diltiazem and verapamil have preferential effects in the heart in the order of the conduction systems and contractile myocardial cells in addition to their peripheral effects. **Because of these negative inotropic and chronotropic effects, these agents are usually only used for select presentations of hypertensive crisis.**"

**Hypertension is NOT an indication.** [PI] lists two: "Temporary control of rapid ventricular rate in atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter" and "Rapid conversion of paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardias (PSVT) to sinus rhythm."

**Bolus dosing [PI], verbatim:**
> "The initial dose of diltiazem hydrochloride injection should be **0.25 mg/kg actual body weight as a bolus administered over 2 minutes** (20 mg is a reasonable dose for the average patient). If response is inadequate, a second dose may be administered **after 15 minutes**. The second bolus dose… should be **0.35 mg/kg** actual body weight administered over 2 minutes (25 mg is a reasonable dose for the average patient)."

**Infusion dosing [PI], verbatim:**
> "The recommended initial infusion rate… is **10 mg/h**. Some patients may maintain response to an initial rate of **5 mg/h**. The infusion rate may be increased in **5 mg/h increments up to 15 mg/h** as needed… The infusion may be maintained for **up to 24 hours**." And: "infusion duration exceeding 24 hours and infusion rates exceeding 15 mg/h are not recommended" (because "diltiazem shows dose-dependent, non-linear pharmacokinetics").

**Onset and duration [PI], verbatim:**
> "Following administration of one or two intravenous bolus doses…, **response usually occurs within 3 minutes and maximal heart rate reduction generally occurs in 2 to 7 minutes. Heart rate reduction may last from 1 to 3 hours.** If hypotension occurs, it is generally short-lived, but may last from 1 to 3 hours."
> After stopping an infusion: "**heart rate reduction may last from 0.5 hours to more than 10 hours (median duration 7 hours).** Hypotension, if it occurs, may be similarly persistent."
That median 7-hour offset after an infusion is the number most people get wrong — diltiazem is **not** a quick-off drip.

**Half-life [PI].** "approximately **3.4 hours**" after a single IV dose, rising to "4.1 to 4.9 hours" on constant infusion.

**Where it fits.** The right drug when hypertension travels with **AF/atrial flutter with RVR or SVT** in a patient with preserved EF — one agent covers both, and its modest arterial vasodilation lowers BP without the reflex tachycardia of a pure dihydropyridine. It is not a first-line hypertensive-emergency agent and should not be chosen for BP alone.

**Contraindications [PI], verbatim (complete):** "1. Patients with sick sinus syndrome except in the presence of a functioning ventricular pacemaker. 2. Patients with second- or third-degree AV block except in the presence of a functioning ventricular pacemaker. 3. Patients with severe hypotension or cardiogenic shock. 4. Patients who have demonstrated hypersensitivity to the drug. 5. **Intravenous diltiazem and intravenous beta-blockers should not be administered together or in close proximity (within a few hours).** 6. Patients with atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter associated with an accessory bypass tract such as in WPW syndrome or short PR syndrome. 7. Patients with ventricular tachycardia."
Item 5 is a **contraindication, not a caution** — relevant on a card where esmolol/labetalol and diltiazem might both be in play. Item 7's rationale, verbatim: "Administration of other calcium channel blockers to patients with wide complex tachycardia (QRS ≥ 0.12 seconds) has resulted in hemodynamic deterioration and ventricular fibrillation."

**Caution in reduced EF — state this accurately.** The label is **softer** than common teaching. Verbatim: "hemodynamic studies in humans with normal ventricular function and in patients with a compromised myocardium… have not shown a reduction in cardiac index nor consistent negative effects on contractility (dp/dt)… Experience with the use of diltiazem hydrochloride injection in patients with impaired ventricular function is limited. **Caution should be exercised** when using the drug in such patients." Also: "in rare instances, worsening of congestive heart failure has been reported in patients with preexisting impaired ventricular function." **There is no reduced-EF contraindication for IV diltiazem**; the only EF-related contraindication is for **oral** diltiazem in acute MI with radiographic pulmonary congestion. Do not overstate it on the card — but the practical teaching (avoid in decompensated systolic HF) remains sound.

**Symptomatic hypotension** occurred in 3.2% of trial patients, requiring fluids or Trendelenburg. **No boxed warning.**

**Card verdict: ADD ONE LINE TO THE FLOOR TABLE** under rate-control-plus-hypertension. Not an ICU BP-titration agent. Suggested line: *Diltiazem IV 0.25 mg/kg (≈20 mg) over 2 min, may repeat 0.35 mg/kg (≈25 mg) at 15 min; infusion 5–15 mg/h, max 24 h. Onset ~3 min, peak 2–7 min, bolus effect 1–3 h; **after an infusion HR effect persists a median 7 h**. For AF-RVR with HTN. Contraindicated with IV beta-blockers, WPW, VT; caution in low EF.*

## 12. FUROSEMIDE / BUMETANIDE (IV) — adjunct, not a primary agent

### Furosemide injection
**Labels:** PLR format (Hospira, rev. 10/2024) https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=aaced7a8-c3d7-4d66-8c07-aa407b8b3f35 · old format (Heritage/Avet, rev. 2/2025) https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=1b9117d5-0dd9-4577-a1fd-bb0aaf7e68ec

**Class/mechanism.** Loop diuretic — inhibits the Na-K-2Cl cotransporter in the thick ascending limb. Also produces acute venodilation before diuresis begins, which is part of its effect in pulmonary edema.

**Hypertension is NOT an IV indication.** [PI] §1 lists only "**Edema** — …associated with heart failure, cirrhosis of the liver, and renal disease, including the nephrotic syndrome" and "**Acute Pulmonary Edema** — …as adjunctive therapy." The hypertension indication is explicitly route-restricted on the oral label, whose own wording is "**Oral** furosemide may be used in adults for the treatment of hypertension" (https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=6a0fb397-63ae-4fce-bddf-16a91b1b7a2f).

**Dosing [PI], verbatim.**
- Acute pulmonary edema: "The usual initial dose of furosemide is **40 mg injected slowly intravenously (over 1 minute to 2 minutes)**. If a satisfactory response does not occur within **1 hour**, increase the dose to **80 mg** injected slowly intravenously."
- Edema: "The usual initial dose of furosemide is **20 mg to 40 mg** given as a single dose… If needed, administer another dose in the same manner **2 hours later** or increase the dose. The dose may be raised by 20 mg and administered not sooner than 2 hours after the previous dose until the desired diuretic effect has been obtained."
- **Rate limit, verbatim:** high-dose parenteral therapy should be given "as a controlled intravenous infusion at a rate **not greater than 4 mg/min**."
- **Adult maximum: not stated.** The label gives no adult ceiling (pediatric only: "Doses greater than 6 mg/kg body weight are not recommended").
- **There is no labeled continuous-infusion regimen.** The common 5–20 mg/h drip is not in FDA labeling — only the 4 mg/min ceiling for high-dose therapy.

**Onset / peak / duration [PI], verbatim:** "The onset of diuresis following intravenous administration is **within 5 minutes**… The **peak effect occurs within the first half hour**. The duration of diuretic effect is **approximately 2 hours**." Terminal half-life ~2 hours.

**Boxed warning — depends on the manufacturer's label format.** Old-format labels carry it, verbatim: "**WARNING** — Furosemide is a potent diuretic which, if given in excessive amounts, can lead to a profound diuresis with water and electrolyte depletion. Therefore, careful medical supervision is required and dose and dose schedule must be adjusted to the individual patient's needs." The **PLR-converted labels have no boxed warning**; that content moved into §5.1. Safest phrasing for a card: "boxed warning on older-format labels; a Warning in PLR labels."

**Ototoxicity [PI], verbatim:** "Cases of tinnitus and reversible or irreversible hearing impairment and deafness have been reported… usually…associated with **rapid injection**, severe renal impairment, the use of higher than recommended doses, hypoproteinemia or concomitant therapy with aminoglycoside antibiotics, ethacrynic acid, or other ototoxic drugs."

**Sulfonamide cross-reactivity — the teaching has changed.** The old-format label says "Patients allergic to sulfonamides may also be allergic to furosemide." **That sentence has been deleted in the PLR conversion**; the PLR contraindication is narrowly "a history of hypersensitivity to furosemide." This reflects the evidence that antibiotic-sulfonamide allergy does not reliably cross-react with non-antibiotic sulfonamides. Do not repeat the old blanket claim.

**Contraindications [PI], verbatim:** "Furosemide Injection is contraindicated in patients with **anuria**." and "…in patients with a **history of hypersensitivity to furosemide**."

**Compatibility caveat [PI], verbatim:** furosemide is "a buffered alkaline solution with a pH of about 9 and the drug may precipitate at pH values below 7… Acid solutions, including other parenteral medications (e.g., **labetalol**, ciprofloxacin, amrinone, milrinone) must not be administered concurrently in the same infusion because they may cause precipitation of the furosemide." Directly relevant to this card — do not run labetalol and furosemide through the same line.

### Bumetanide injection
**Label:** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=681b5e9d-e2c7-4f29-aaad-e06d4a528c11 (Hikma, rev. 7/2026)

**Dosing [PI], verbatim:** "The usual initial dose is **0.5 to 1 mg** intravenously or intramuscularly. Intravenous administration should be given over a period of **1 to 2 minutes**. If the response to an initial dose is deemed insufficient, a second or third dose may be given at intervals of **2 to 3 hours**, but should not exceed a daily dosage of **10 mg**." (The oral label uses 4–5 hour intervals — do not conflate.)

**IV:PO potency ratio — this is in FDA labeling, not folklore.** [PI] §Clinical Pharmacology, verbatim: "Pharmacological and clinical studies have shown that **1 mg bumetanide has a diuretic potency equivalent to approximately 40 mg furosemide**." The same sentence appears on the Hikma injection, Sagent injection and Bumex tablet labels. (Caveat: the ototoxicity section of the same label says "about 40 to 60 times" — the label is not internally consistent; use 1:40.)
Bumetanide has **near-complete oral bioavailability**, so bumetanide IV:PO is ~1:1 — [PI]: "Almost equal diuretic response occurs after oral and parenteral administration of bumetanide." Furosemide, by contrast, is ~50% orally bioavailable (hence the familiar furosemide IV:PO of 1:2).

**Onset / peak / duration [PI], verbatim:** "**Diuresis starts within minutes following an intravenous injection and reaches maximum levels within 15 to 30 minutes.**" **The injection label states no IV duration** — it says only "rapid onset and short duration of action." The familiar 4–6 h duration comes from the **oral** Bumex label ("At usual doses (1 mg to 2 mg) diuresis is largely complete within 4 hours; with higher doses, the diuretic action lasts for 4 to 6 hours"). Attribute it as extrapolated, not as an IV figure. Half-life "between 1 and 1½ hours."

**Boxed warning — PRESENT, verbatim:** "**WARNING** — Bumetanide Injection, USP is a potent diuretic which, if given in excessive amounts, can lead to a profound diuresis with water and electrolyte depletion. Therefore, careful medical supervision is required, and dose and dosage schedule have to be adjusted to the individual patient's needs."

**Contraindications [PI]:** anuria; hepatic coma; severe electrolyte depletion until corrected; hypersensitivity. Useful for the sulfa question, [PI] verbatim: "**Successful treatment with bumetanide following instances of allergic reactions to furosemide suggests a lack of cross-sensitivity.**"

### Where loops fit on this card

**Where they fit conceptually.** Loop diuretics are an **adjunct** in hypertensive emergency, not a BP-titration agent, and are appropriate in exactly two situations:
1. **Volume-mediated hypertension** — the hypertensive patient who is genuinely volume overloaded (ESRD between sessions, decompensated HF, oliguric AKI, high-salt states). Here vasodilators alone chase the number without fixing the cause.
2. **Acute pulmonary edema / SCAPE** — as an adjunct to nitroglycerin and NIV.

**The important caveat.** Many hypertensive-emergency patients are **not** volume overloaded; several are intravascularly depleted from pressure natriuresis, and a loop diuretic in that patient causes further RAAS activation and worsens the hypertension. In SCAPE specifically, the pathophysiology is **fluid redistribution, not fluid excess** — high-dose nitroglycerin and NIV are the primary therapy and diuretics play a secondary role. Do not reflex-order furosemide for a high BP reading.

**Card verdict: WORTH A ONE-LINE FOOTNOTE under acute pulmonary edema**, explicitly framed as an adjunct with the "only if truly volume overloaded" caveat. Not a table row with onset/duration alongside titratable drips.

## 13. HYDRALAZINE (IV) — card has it in the FLOOR table

**Card (Table 4):** `IV 5-20mg q15-30min until effect seen, then PO, careful of unpredictable response (10-40mg IM if no access) | onset 10-20min | duration 1-4h | Eclampsia. Otherwise, no specific indications`

**Class/mechanism.** Direct-acting peripheral **arteriolar** vasodilator (venous capacitance largely unaffected), so it drops afterload while triggering baroreflex sympathetic activation.

**Dosing [PI], verbatim.** "**The usual dose is 20 to 40 mg, repeated as necessary.**" "Certain patients (especially those with marked renal damage) may require a lower dose." "Hydralazine hydrochloride injection **should be used only when the drug cannot be given orally**."
**Note:** the label dose (20–40 mg) is *higher* than modern practice. **[CCSAP]** Table 1-5: "IV bolus: **10–20 mg**; IM: 10–40 mg q30min PRN." Obstetric protocols use **5–10 mg q20–30 min**. The card's 5–20 mg q15–30 min is the safest of these and is the right number to print.

**Onset [PI], verbatim.** BP "may begin to fall within a few minutes after injection, with the **average maximal decrease occurring in 10 to 80 minutes**." **[CCSAP]** Table 1-5: IV onset **10 min**, IM onset 20 min.
**Time to peak.** **10 to 80 minutes** — an 8-fold spread, and this single number is the core of the problem with the drug.

**TRUE duration — resolving the 1–4 h vs up-to-12 h disagreement.**
- The FDA injection label **does not state a duration at all**, and does not give a plasma half-life.
- **[CCSAP]** Table 1-5: IV **1–4 hr** (IM 2–6 hr). This is the figure the card uses and is the fair central estimate.
- **[IBCC]:** IV duration 2–4 h, but adds that "vascular effects may persist 24–100 hours" and that half-life is "context-dependent; prolonged in renal failure."
- Tertiary and pharmacokinetic sources cite hypotensive effects persisting **up to 12 hours** against a plasma half-life of only ~3–7 h. The mechanism is tissue sequestration: hydralazine "can be found in high concentrations in liver, kidneys, lungs, adrenals and **arteries**" (WHO/IPCS monograph, https://www.inchem.org/documents/pims/pharm/hydral.htm), so the **vascular effect outlasts the plasma level**. Same source on oral dosing: maximum hypotensive effect at 2–4 h "and may persist for up to 24 hours."
- **Acetylator phenotype** is the other axis. Hydralazine is cleared by N-acetyltransferase-2; **slow acetylators** (roughly half of white and Black populations, higher in some Middle Eastern populations) reach much higher exposure — oral fractional availability 0.30–0.35 vs 0.10–0.16 in rapid acetylators. **Accuracy caveat:** that difference is largely a *first-pass* effect, so it is most pronounced for oral dosing; IV bypasses first pass and the phenotype effect is correspondingly smaller. Still relevant to the intern who gives IV hydralazine and then converts to PO at the same nominal dose. Neither the injection label nor the tablet label mentions acetylator status in its dosing section.
- Renal impairment prolongs it further — the injection label's only dose-reduction advice is "Certain patients (especially those with marked renal damage) may require a lower dose."

**Verdict on duration:** "1–4 h" is a defensible *median*, but it is not the whole truth — the honest statement is **"1–4 h typically, but can persist 8–12 h; longer in renal impairment."** For a card whose whole point is warning against hydralazine, the longer tail is the more useful number. The more damning number is the **peak**, not the duration: **10 to 80 minutes** to maximal effect.

**Why it is discouraged for titration — sourced quote.**
> "Intravenous hydralazine is a potent vasodilator with a rapid onset of action that has an **unpredictable effect on blood pressure and unpredictable dose response**."
> — Campbell P, Baker WL, Bendel SD, White WB. *Intravenous hydralazine for blood pressure management in the hospitalized patient: its use is often unjustified.* J Am Soc Hypertens. 2011;5(6):473-477. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21890447/ (in that series, 94 patients received 201 IV hydralazine doses and **only 4 patients (2%) had an urgent hypertensive condition**).

> "However, caution should be used, given the agent's suboptimal PK profile, **unpredictable duration of effect on blood pressure**, and reports of inducing rebound tachycardia."
> — **[CCSAP]**, https://www.accp.com/docs/bookstore/ccsap/ccsap2018b1_sample.pdf

> "Hydralazine generally isn't preferred since the response can be erratic."
> — **[IBCC]**, https://emcrit.org/ibcc/htn/

**[CCSAP]** Table 1-7 summarizes the three failure modes: "Can result in prolonged hypotension, given longer half-life / Risk of reflex tachycardia / Headaches, lupus-like syndrome."

**The mechanism of the problem, stated plainly.** You give a dose, nothing happens for 10–80 minutes, so someone gives another dose; then both land at once and the patient overshoots for hours, with no way to reverse it. Meanwhile the baroreflex tachycardia raises myocardial oxygen demand — which is why the label contraindicates it in **coronary artery disease**.

**Contraindications [PI], verbatim:** "Hypersensitivity to hydralazine; **coronary artery disease**; mitral valvular rheumatic heart disease."
**Warnings [PI]:** "Myocardial stimulation produced by hydralazine can cause anginal attacks"; drug-induced lupus — "hydralazine may produce a clinical picture simulating systemic lupus erythematosus"; "**Blood pressure should be checked frequently.**"

**Where it is legitimately preferred.** **Severe hypertension of pregnancy / preeclampsia / eclampsia** — one of the three ACOG-endorsed first-line agents (with IV labetalol and immediate-release oral nifedipine), where the comparative trials show no clear superiority and agent choice rests on contraindications and clinician familiarity **[CCSAP]**. The card's "Eclampsia. Otherwise, no specific indications" is exactly right.

**Card verdict on placement: CORRECT — keep it off the ICU table.** It is the antithesis of a titratable drip. Two suggested strengthenings: (a) change the duration to "1–4 h, up to 8–12 h"; (b) add "avoid in CAD (label contraindication, reflex tachycardia)."

## 14. LABETALOL vs ESMOLOL IN AORTIC DISSECTION

**What the guideline actually says.** The **2022 ACC/AHA aortic disease guideline** gives a **Class 1** recommendation for IV beta-blockade as initial therapy and **names esmolol, metoprolol, or labetalol without ranking them**. Targets: "Patients with AAS should be treated to a systolic BP <120 mm Hg or to the lowest BP that maintains end-organ perfusion, and to a **heart rate of 60-80 bpm**"; "Unless contraindicated, intravenous beta-blockers are recommended as initial management, and vasodilators if BP is not adequately controlled with beta-blockers." https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/ten-points-to-remember/2022/11/01/12/21/2022-guideline-on-aortic-disease-2-gl-ad

**So there is no guideline preference.** Anyone who tells an intern "esmolol is the right answer" is stating a practice preference, not a guideline. The honest teaching points are these:

**The sequencing rule is the non-negotiable part.** Beta-blocker **first**, vasodilator **second**. A vasodilator given alone drops SBP but triggers reflex tachycardia and a rise in **dP/dt** (the rate of rise of aortic pressure), which is the actual force propagating the dissection flap. **[CCSAP]** Table 1-7 states the rule for both esmolol and metoprolol: "Should be used in conjunction with an arterial vasodilator for BP management in aortic dissection (**initiate esmolol first because of its delayed onset relative to vasodilators** such as sodium nitroprusside)."

**Where they genuinely differ [CCSAP] Table 1-7:**
- **Labetalol:** "**May be used as monotherapy in acute aortic dissection**" — because its alpha-1 blockade supplies the vasodilation, so one drug achieves both the HR and SBP target. Simpler, one pump. Cost: a 2–6 h (up to 18 h) duration and a bolus-friendly/infusion-awkward PK profile, so overshoot is hard to undo.
- **Esmolol:** requires a **second agent** (nicardipine or clevidipine) for the SBP target, because beta-1 selectivity gives no vasodilation. Cost: two pumps. Benefit: a 9-minute half-life and 10–20 min offset, so if the patient becomes hypotensive — in a population that may be actively bleeding, tamponading or heading to the OR — you can withdraw the beta-blockade in minutes rather than hours.

**Practical synthesis for the card.** Both are Class 1. **Esmolol is preferred when the patient is hemodynamically fragile or heading imminently to the OR** (reversibility in minutes, organ-independent metabolism in a patient with malperfusion of liver or kidney). **Labetalol is preferred when you want a single agent and the patient is stable** and you can get HR <60 and SBP <120 from one drip. The card's Table 2 entry — "IV BB (labetalol, esmolol), followed by vasodilator (nicardipine/clevidipine)" — is **CONFIRMED CORRECT** as written and correctly captures the sequencing rule.

## 15. IV VERAPAMIL, IV URAPIDIL, IV CLONIDINE — one line each

- **IV verapamil.** Non-dihydropyridine CCB; US label indication is **supraventricular arrhythmia, not hypertension**. Older data show a continuous infusion of 5–10 mg/h lowers MAP ~20%, but its negative inotropy exceeds diltiazem's and it has no advantage over a dihydropyridine for BP. **[CCSAP]** groups it with diltiazem as "usually only used for select presentations of hypertensive crisis." **Verdict: no role on an inpatient hypertensive-emergency card.** Rate control only, and diltiazem is the better-tolerated choice.

- **IV urapidil.** Peripheral alpha-1 antagonist with central 5-HT1A agonism; widely used in Europe (one of the three commonest IV agents there, with nitroglycerin and clonidine). Trials show it is as effective as nitroprusside with fewer adverse effects, and better than sublingual nifedipine. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9310807/ . **Verdict: not available in the US — mention only if you read European literature.**

- **IV clonidine.** **Not available as an IV product in the US.** The only US parenteral clonidine is **Duraclon, labeled for continuous epidural infusion for cancer pain** (https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=8c126bb8-732a-4949-8754-2f50b5543638); it is not an IV antihypertensive. IV clonidine is used in Europe. What *is* relevant on a US card: **oral clonidine withdrawal causes rebound hypertensive crisis** — treat by restarting clonidine, add phentolamine if needed, and **do not give an unopposed beta-blocker**. **Verdict: no IV entry; keep the withdrawal caveat.**

### A cross-cutting point worth putting on the card

Three of the four floor-common IV agents interns reach for are **not FDA-approved intravenously for hypertension at all**:

| Agent | Approved IV indication | Hypertension approved IV? |
|---|---|---|
| Metoprolol tartrate | Acute MI, hemodynamically stable | **No** (oral only) |
| Diltiazem | AF/AFL rate control; PSVT conversion | **No** |
| Furosemide | Edema; acute pulmonary edema (adjunct) | **No** (oral only — label says "*Oral* furosemide") |
| Hydralazine | Severe hypertension (when PO not possible) | Yes |
| Labetalol | Severe hypertension | Yes |

That is not an argument against ever using them — it is an argument for knowing that when you push IV metoprolol for a BP of 190, you are using a rate drug off-label on a patient whose problem is vasoconstriction.
