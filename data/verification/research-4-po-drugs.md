# TABLE 4 — Oral Antihypertensives (Floor): Source Verification

Verified 2026-09-21. Primary sources: FDA prescribing information via DailyMed; ACOG Practice Bulletin 222 (2020) and Committee Opinion 692 (2017); 2017 and 2025 ACC/AHA hypertension guidelines; named trials.

---

## 0. THREE CROSS-CUTTING CORRECTIONS THAT AFFECT THE WHOLE TABLE

### 0a. "Onset" on this card is mostly the wrong number
For a floor card the number that changes behavior is **time to peak effect**, because that is when it is safe to re-dose. Several of the card's "onset" figures are either (a) the IV figure mistakenly applied to the PO form (labetalol), (b) the peak mislabeled as onset (captopril), or (c) not in the label at all (hydralazine, amlodipine). Recommend the table carry **onset / peak / full effect** rather than onset / duration.

### 0b. The card is missing "time to full antihypertensive effect" everywhere
This is the field that prevents dose-stacking. Label-sourced values:

| Drug | Full antihypertensive effect |
|---|---|
| Labetalol PO | 24–72 h (max steady-state BP response on BID dosing) |
| Captopril | "several weeks of therapy may be required" |
| Lisinopril | 2–4 weeks |
| Amlodipine | steady state 7–8 days; titrate over 7–14 days |
| Nifedipine ER | titrate over 7–14 days |
| HCTZ | not stated (pivotal trial window 4 weeks) |
| Metoprolol (both salts) | max effect of any given dose apparent after 1 week |
| Carvedilol | "full antihypertensive effect … seen within 7 to 14 days" |
| Losartan | present within 1 week, maximal 3–6 weeks |
| Valsartan | substantially present 2 weeks, maximal 4 weeks |
| Diltiazem ER | max antihypertensive effect usually by 14 days |
| Verapamil ER | evident within the first week |
| Spironolactone | titrate at 2-week intervals |
| Torsemide | near-maximal after 4–6 weeks |
| Methyldopa | smooth response in 12–24 h once at effective dose |
| Clonidine patch | 2–3 days |
| Minoxidil | 7 d at 10 mg/d; 5 d at 20 mg/d; 3 d at 40 mg/d |

### 0c. The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline now argues against the card's implied PRN model
The 2025 guideline replaces "hypertensive urgency" with **severe hypertension (BP >180/120 mm Hg)** and, for adults with SBP >180 or DBP >120 **without acute target-organ damage**, "advises against the intermittent use of intravenous or oral antihypertensive medications solely for the purpose of acute BP reduction."
- 2025 guideline: https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/HYP.0000000000000249
- Open-access summary quoting the recommendation: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12995957/

Supporting evidence (see §11 for detail): Mohandas, *Hypertension* 2021 (PRN antihypertensives → AKI OR 1.24, ischemic stroke OR 8.50, in-hospital death OR 2.36); Gaynor, *Ther Adv Cardiovasc Dis* 2018 (36% of PRN doses given below the severe-HTN threshold; 41% of those patients were not even on their home regimen); Anderson, *JAMA Intern Med* 2019 (intensifying antihypertensives at discharge in older adults → harm without BP benefit at 1 year).

## 1. LABETALOL — oral

**Class:** non-selective beta blocker with selective alpha-1 blockade (beta:alpha ≈ 3:1 PO, ≈ 7:1 IV).
**Label:** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=b4c13317-88eb-48f2-aba2-50f01c8ff938

**Dosing (label, verbatim):**
- "The recommended initial dosage is 100 mg twice daily" — alone or added to a diuretic.
- "After 2 or 3 days, using standing blood pressure as an indicator, dosage may be titrated in increments of 100 mg b.i.d. every 2 or 3 days."
- "The usual maintenance dosage … is between 200 and 400 mg twice daily."
- "Patients with severe hypertension may require from 1,200 mg to 2,400 mg per day."

**Kinetics / effect timing (label):**
- Peak effect of a single oral dose: **2 to 4 hours**. No onset figure is given.
- Duration: "at least 8 hours following single oral doses of 100 mg and more than 12 hours following single oral doses of 300 mg."
- Full effect: "The maximum, steady-state blood pressure response upon oral, twice-a-day dosing occurs within **24 to 72 hours**." Plasma steady state by ~day 3. Plasma t½ 6–8 h.
- Independent PD study (Serlin, *Br J Clin Pharmacol* 1979, n=dose-ranging 100/200/400 mg): "Labetalol caused a fall in blood pressure within 2 h" and "this fall which was dose-related was maximal by 3 h." https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1429443/

**Cautions / contraindications (label):** bronchial asthma, overt cardiac failure, greater-than-first-degree heart block, cardiogenic shock, severe bradycardia. Boxed-level warning on rare **severe hepatocellular injury** (necrosis and death reported) — check LFTs at the first sign of liver dysfunction. Postural hypotension most likely **2–4 h after a dose**. Taper over 1–2 weeks on discontinuation. Transient scalp tingling on initiation.

**Renal:** no dose adjustment — "In patients with decreased hepatic or renal function, the elimination half-life of labetalol is not altered." Bioavailability *is* increased in hepatic impairment (less first-pass) — use caution and lower doses in cirrhosis.

**Preferred in:** aortic dissection and acute stroke (IV form); pregnancy — ACOG PB 222 gives oral labetalol **200 mg q12h, increase to 800 mg q8–12h, max 2,400 mg/d** for expectant management.

### Verdicts
| Card claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Start 100 mg q8–12h | **CORRECTED (partial).** Label initial dose is **100 mg BID**, not q8h. TID is used clinically but is off-label as an *initial* dose and biases toward stacking. |
| Max 2,400 mg/d | **CONFIRMED.** (Also confirmed by ACOG PB 222.) |
| Onset 20 min | **CORRECTED.** 20 min is not an oral figure. Oral BP fall begins within ~1–2 h and peaks 2–4 h. The card's own IV row (5–10 min) is the fast number. |
| Duration 8–12 h | **CONFIRMED** (≥8 h at 100 mg, >12 h at 300 mg). |
| *(missing)* Full effect | **ADD: 24–72 h.** |

## 2. HYDRALAZINE — oral

**Class:** direct arteriolar vasodilator (no venodilation).
**Label (tablets):** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=f9f56326-6d72-47d3-89ac-3625b8a6d713
**Label (injection):** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=dca82025-a4cd-4856-aa19-bdf091bf8973

**Dosing (label, verbatim):** "Start with 10 mg four times daily for the first 2–4 days, increase to 25 mg four times daily for the balance of the first week. For the second and subsequent weeks, increase dosage to 50 mg four times daily." Maintenance: lowest effective dose. "In a few resistant patients, up to 300 mg … daily may be required." Label elsewhere refers to "the maximum daily human dose of 200 to 300 mg"; pediatric max is 200 mg/day.

**Kinetics (label):** peak plasma **1–2 h**; t½ **3–7 h**; 87% protein bound; polymorphic acetylation — "slow acetylators generally have higher plasma levels … and require lower doses."
**The oral label gives NO onset and NO duration of antihypertensive effect.** The injection label is the only place a timing figure appears: "The average maximal decrease in blood pressure usually occurs **10–80 minutes** after administration of hydralazine hydrochloride injection."

**Contraindications (label — these are the ones people forget):** hypersensitivity; **coronary artery disease**; **mitral valvular rheumatic heart disease**.

**Warnings:** "Myocardial stimulation produced by hydralazine hydrochloride can cause anginal attacks and ECG changes of myocardial ischemia." Reflex renin release → angiotensin II → aldosterone → **sodium and water retention** (pseudotolerance). Peripheral neuritis (paresthesia/numbness/tingling) → add pyridoxine. Caution in advanced renal damage.

**Lupus-like syndrome:** label — "In a few patients hydralazine may produce a clinical picture simulating systemic lupus erythematosus including glomerulonephritis"; "The incidence of toxic reactions, particularly the L.E. cell syndrome, is **high in the group of patients receiving large doses**"; "Symptoms and signs usually regress when the drug is discontinued **but residua have been detected many years later**."
Dose threshold from the literature: risk rises above **200 mg/day** — incidence in women ~**19.4% at 3 years on 200 mg/day** vs **5.5% at 3 years on 50–100 mg/day**. Other risk factors: slow acetylator status (29/30 patients in one drug-induced-lupus series were slow acetylators), female sex, therapy >3 months, HLA-DR4. Reference: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12122051/ ; case series of low-dose disease: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5591929/

**Why it is a poor monotherapy choice:** arteriolar-only vasodilation triggers baroreflex tachycardia and increased cardiac output (→ angina/ischemia, contraindicated in CAD) plus renin-driven salt and water retention, both of which blunt the antihypertensive effect within days. The label's own combination advice is hydralazine + a thiazide and/or a beta blocker. In HFrEF it is only used **paired with a nitrate** (see §8).

**The specific problem with scheduled/PRN hydralazine in hospital:** it is the single most-ordered PRN antihypertensive (90.9% of 573 PRN doses in Gaynor 2018; 53% of PRN doses in Mohandas 2021), its BP response is unpredictable in magnitude and timing (onset 10–80 min IV), and its duration outlasts the nursing re-check interval, so stacked doses land together. It is associated with abrupt >25% SBP drops (OR 2.05), AKI (OR 1.24), ischemic stroke (OR 8.50) and in-hospital death (OR 2.36). It also substitutes for the actual fix — 40.8% of patients receiving PRN antihypertensives were not on their home regimen at all.

|---|---|

| *(missing)* Contraindicated in CAD | **ADD — this is a labeled contraindication.** |

## 3. CAPTOPRIL

**Class:** ACE inhibitor (sulfhydryl-containing; shortest-acting oral ACEi).
**Label:** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=5b44ab6e-6cd9-4e12-b9be-d2e4d954acb5

**Dosing (label):** initial **25 mg BID or TID**; after 1–2 weeks increase to 50 mg BID–TID; further to 100–150 mg per dose usually with a diuretic; **"the maximum daily dose of 450 mg … should not be exceeded."**
**Food:** "The presence of food in the gastrointestinal tract reduces absorption by about **30 to 40 percent**; captopril therefore should be given **one hour before meals**." (Practical floor point: a captopril dose handed out with a tray is a materially smaller dose.)
**Renal:** reduced initial dose, smaller increments, and slow titration "at one-to two-week intervals."

### The onset question — resolved
- **FDA label:** "Reductions of blood pressure are usually **maximal 60 to 90 minutes** after oral administration of an individual dose." This is the **PEAK**, explicitly, not the onset. The label gives no onset number, and states the duration is "dose related."
- **The card's "onset 30–90 min" is the label's peak figure relabeled as onset.**
- **Your recollection is correct.** Captopril's pharmacodynamic onset is faster: after 25 mg PO, plasma angiotensin II reaches its nadir at **30–40 minutes** (Semple, *Cardiovasc Drugs Ther* 1987;1:45–50, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2856460/). Standard clinical references cite BP onset **15–30 min**, peak **1–1.5 h**, consistent with the label's 60–90 min peak.
- **2017 ACC/AHA guideline:** it does **not** contain an oral-agent onset/duration table. Its table structure is **Table 18 "Oral Antihypertensive Drugs"** (drug class, usual dose range, daily frequency, comments — **no onset, no duration**), **Table 19 "Intravenous Antihypertensive Drugs for Treatment of Hypertensive Emergencies"** (this is the table that carries onset and duration, and it is IV-only: nitroprusside, nicardipine, clevidipine, esmolol, labetalol, hydralazine, nitroglycerin, fenoldopam, phentolamine, enalaprilat), and **Table 20** (IV drugs by comorbidity). So **no onset or duration figure for captopril — or for any oral agent — can be attributed to the 2017 guideline.** https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/HYP.0000000000000065
- **Duration:** the label says only that it is dose-related and that "the antihypertensive effects persist for a longer period of time than does demonstrable inhibition of circulating ACE." TID dosing (t½ <2 h for parent drug) is consistent with a ~6–8 h effect; the card's figure is reasonable but is convention, not label text.

**Full effect:** "several weeks of therapy may be required" for maximal antihypertensive effect.

**Cautions:** boxed fetal toxicity — stop as soon as pregnancy detected. Contraindicated with prior ACEi angioedema, with sacubitril/valsartan, and with aliskiren in diabetes. Angioedema can cause fatal airway obstruction (higher rate in Black patients). Neutropenia/agranulocytosis — monitor in renal impairment and collagen vascular disease. Hyperkalemia, especially with CKD or K-sparing agents. Acute renal failure with bilateral renal artery stenosis.

### Scleroderma renal crisis — the real regimen
- **Why captopril specifically:** rapid onset and short duration of action permit **rapid dose escalation and rapid back-off** if BP overshoots. This is the only setting where the short half-life is an advantage. (UK Scleroderma Study Group guidelines, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27749244/ ; Prisant, *J Clin Hypertens* 2003, https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1524-6175.2003.01928.x)
- **Dosing is far higher than the card's 12.5–25 q8h:** start **6.25–12.5 mg**, escalate in **12.5–25 mg increments every 4–8 hours** to goal; **maximum 300–450 mg/day**, usually divided TID. Target: lower SBP by **~20 mm Hg per 24 h**, reaching ~120/70 within **72 h**, without hypotension.
- **Continue the ACEi even as creatinine rises.** Early ACEi converts oliguric to non-oliguric failure; 1-year survival ~76% with ACEi vs ~15% without (Steen, *Ann Intern Med* 1990;113:352, https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/0003-4819-113-5-352). Therapy is **lifelong regardless of whether dialysis is required** — a substantial fraction recover enough function to come off dialysis over 6–18 months.

### Verdicts
| Card claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| 12.5–25 mg q8h | **CONFIRMED as a conservative inpatient start**, but below the label's initial 25 mg BID–TID. Card omits the **450 mg/d max**. |
| "ensure q8h not TID" | **CONFIRMED and worth keeping** — captopril's short duration means clustered TID dosing leaves a long uncovered gap. |
| Onset 30–90 min | **CORRECTED.** 60–90 min is the label's **peak**. Onset is ~15–30 min (ANG II nadir 30–40 min). |
| Duration 6–8 h | **CONFIRMED by convention**, not stated in the label ("dose related"). |
| Indication: scleroderma renal crisis | **CONFIRMED but the dose on the card is wrong for that indication** — SRC uses 6.25–12.5 mg escalated q4–8 h to 300–450 mg/d. |
| *(missing)* Take 1 h before meals | **ADD** — food cuts absorption 30–40%. |
| *(missing)* Renal dosing | **ADD** — lower start, slower titration. |

## 4. LISINOPRIL

**Class:** long-acting ACE inhibitor (no hepatic activation required, not protein bound, renally cleared).
**Label:** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=7885537f-88b2-4d39-bcd2-cad9b2e5fbe5

**Dosing (label):**
- Hypertension: **initial 10 mg once daily**; usual range **20–40 mg/day** as a single dose; "Doses up to 80 mg have been used but do not appear to give greater effect."
- On a diuretic / volume- or salt-depleted: **5 mg** initial, observed ≥2 h until BP stabilizes (or stop the diuretic 2–3 days first).
- Heart failure: start **5 mg** daily; increase **by no more than 10 mg at intervals of no less than 2 weeks**; range 5–40 mg/day.
- **Renal:** CrCl >30 → 10 mg/d; CrCl 10–30 → **5 mg/d**; CrCl <10 or dialysis → **2.5 mg/d**.

**Effect timing (label, verbatim):** "onset of antihypertensive activity was seen at **one hour** after oral administration," "peak reduction of blood pressure achieved by **6 hours**," antihypertensive effect still "observed 24 hours after dosing," and "In some patients achievement of optimal blood pressure reduction may require **two to four weeks** of therapy."

**Cautions:** boxed fetal toxicity. Contraindicated with prior ACEi angioedema. Angioedema (higher rate in Black patients), hyperkalemia (K >5.7 in ~2.2% of hypertensives, 4.8% of HF patients), hypotension (rare in uncomplicated HTN, common in HF/AMI), acute kidney injury with bilateral RAS, cough.

**Preferred in:** diabetes, albuminuria/proteinuria, HFrEF, post-MI — 2025 guideline: ACEi or ARB (not both) recommended when eGFR <60 or albuminuria ≥30 mg/g.

### Verdicts
| Card claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Start 2.5–5 mg qd | **CORRECTED (partial).** Label initial for uncomplicated HTN is **10 mg**. 2.5–5 mg is the correct start only if on a diuretic, volume-depleted, in HF, or CrCl ≤30 — which is most floor patients, so the card's number is defensible but should say *why*. |
| Increase 10 mg q2w | **CORRECTED.** That is the **heart failure** titration rule. For hypertension the label sets no fixed interval — titrate to BP response; expect 2–4 weeks for full effect. |
| Max 40 mg qd | **CONFIRMED** (80 mg gives no greater effect). |
| Onset 1 h | **CONFIRMED verbatim.** |
| Duration 24 h | **CONFIRMED.** Add peak at 6 h. |
| *(missing)* Renal dosing | **ADD: CrCl 10–30 → 5 mg; <10/HD → 2.5 mg.** |
| *(missing)* Full effect | **ADD: 2–4 weeks.** |

## 5. AMLODIPINE

**Class:** long-acting dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker.
**Label (Norvasc):** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=7367289c-b0b0-466a-83e2-558e2985c29f

**Dosing (label):** usual initial **5 mg once daily**; **2.5 mg** for "small, fragile, or elderly individuals, or patients with hepatic insufficiency" or when added to another antihypertensive; "titration should proceed over **7 to 14 days**"; maximum **10 mg once daily**.

**Effect timing (label):**
- Peak plasma concentration **6–12 h** after a dose; bioavailability unaffected by food.
- Terminal t½ **30–50 h**; **steady-state plasma levels reached after 7–8 days** of daily dosing.
- Mechanistically: "a gradual rate of association and dissociation with the receptor binding site, resulting in a **gradual onset of effect**." **No numeric onset is given anywhere in the label.**
- "With chronic once daily oral administration, antihypertensive effectiveness is maintained for at least 24 hours," with "little difference in peak and trough effect."
- No significant change in heart rate or plasma catecholamines with chronic dosing (i.e. **no reflex tachycardia**), and "because of the gradual onset of action, acute hypotension is unlikely."

**Cautions:** worsening angina/MI on starting or increasing the dose in severe obstructive CAD. Dose-related peripheral edema: 1.8% at 2.5 mg, 3.0% at 5 mg, **10.8% at 10 mg** (placebo 0.6%). Symptomatic hypotension in severe aortic stenosis.
**Renal:** "The pharmacokinetics of amlodipine are not significantly influenced by renal impairment" — **no renal adjustment**, and it is not dialyzed. This is one of its main inpatient advantages.
**Hepatic:** lower initial dose (2.5 mg), slower titration.

### Verdicts
| Card claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Start 2.5–5 mg qd | **CONFIRMED.** (Label: 5 mg usual, 2.5 mg for frail/elderly/hepatic/add-on.) |
| Increase 2.5 mg q7d | **CONFIRMED (approximate)** — label says titrate over 7–14 days. |
| Max 10 mg | **CONFIRMED.** |
| **Onset 24–48 h** | **CORRECTED / UNSOURCED.** No onset figure exists in the label. Peak plasma is **6–12 h**, so BP does move on day 1; what takes a week is the *full* effect (steady state 7–8 days, titration interval 7–14 days). Recommend replacing "onset 24–48 h" with "**measurable effect day 1; full effect 7–14 d**." |

| "Requires few days to take effect" | **CONFIRMED directionally but understated** — it is 7–14 days, not a few days. |
| "No specific indications" | **Worth revising.** Amlodipine's distinguishing inpatient feature is that it needs **no renal adjustment**, causes no reflex tachycardia, and is safe in HFrEF (unlike non-DHP CCBs) — making it the default add-on in CKD and in HF patients. |

## 6. NIFEDIPINE — the safety question, resolved

### 6a. Immediate-release nifedipine capsules
**Label:** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=9e5df4e1-be0d-4026-a7b0-3679b1effbde

The label is unambiguous:
- **Hypertension is NOT an approved indication.** Approved for vasospastic angina and chronic stable angina only. "Nifedipine capsules and other immediate-release nifedipine capsules have also been used for the long-term control of essential hypertension, although Nifedipine capsules have not been approved for this purpose."
- **"Nifedipine capsules should not be used for the acute reduction of blood pressure."**
- Dosing (for angina): 10 mg TID; usual 10–20 mg TID; "a single dose should rarely exceed 30 mg"; **"more than 180 mg per day is not recommended."**
- Peak blood levels ~**30 minutes**; t½ ~2 h.
- "There is little difference in relative bioavailability when Nifedipine capsules are given orally and either swallowed whole, bitten and swallowed, or bitten and held sublingually" — i.e. the sublingual route confers no advantage, only loss of control.

**Guideline/regulatory history (non-pregnant adults):** Grossman, Messerli, Grodzicki & Kowey, *JAMA* 1996;276:1328 — "Should a moratorium be placed on sublingual nifedipine capsules given for hypertensive emergencies and pseudoemergencies?" documents cerebrovascular ischemia, acute MI, severe hypotension and conduction disturbances following sublingual nifedipine; the FDA Cardiorenal Advisory Committee declined to approve it for hypertensive emergencies in 1995. https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/article-abstract/409974
Also: Short-acting nifedipine and stroke risk in elderly hypertensives, *Neurology* 2011. https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0b013e318230201a

### 6b. But ACOG does recommend oral IR nifedipine in pregnancy/postpartum
**ACOG Practice Bulletin 222 (2020), Table 3 — "Antihypertensive Agents Used for Urgent Blood Pressure Control in Pregnancy," verbatim:**

| Drug | Dose | Comments | Onset |
|---|---|---|---|
| Labetalol | 10–20 mg IV, then 20–80 mg q10–30 min to max cumulative 300 mg; or infusion 1–2 mg/min | Tachycardia less common; avoid in asthma, preexisting myocardial disease, decompensated cardiac function, heart block, bradycardia | 1–2 min |
| Hydralazine | 5 mg IV or IM, then 5–10 mg IV q20–40 min to max cumulative 20 mg; or infusion 0.5–10 mg/h | Higher/frequent dosing → maternal hypotension, headache, abnormal FHR tracings | 10–20 min |
| **Nifedipine (immediate release)** | **10–20 mg orally, repeat in 20 minutes if needed; then 10–20 mg every 2–6 hours; maximum daily dose 180 mg** | May observe reflex tachycardia and headaches | **5–10 min** |

Text: "Intravenous hydralazine or labetalol and oral nifedipine are the three agents most commonly used for this purpose." Treatment threshold: SBP ≥160 or DBP ≥110 confirmed persistent ≥15 min; agents should be given **within 30–60 minutes**. Source PDF: https://www.preeclampsia.org/frontend/assets/img/advocacy_resource/Gestational_Hypertension_and_Preeclampsia_ACOG_Practice_Bulletin,_Number_222_1605448006.pdf ; ACOG page: https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/practice-bulletin/articles/2020/06/gestational-hypertension-and-preeclampsia

**ACOG Committee Opinion 692 sample order set (oral nifedipine arm), verbatim:** nifedipine 10 mg PO → recheck BP in 20 min → if still above threshold, nifedipine capsules 20 mg PO → recheck in 20 min → if still above threshold, nifedipine 20 mg PO → recheck in 20 min → if still above, labetalol 40 mg IV over 2 min **plus emergency MFM/IM/anesthesia/critical care consultation**. Footnote: **"Capsules should be administered orally and not punctured or otherwise administered sublingually."** https://ilpqc.org/wp-content/docs/htn/ACOGsamplesets.pdf
(CO 692 itself ranks IV labetalol and IV hydralazine first-line with IR nifedipine "when IV access is not available"; PB 222 and the sample order sets treat all three as usable first-line options, and randomized data — Cochrane 2013; Shekhar 2016; Li 2023 — show equal or faster BP control with oral nifedipine. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12453142/)

### 6c. The clean rule for the card
> **IR nifedipine is appropriate for acute-onset severe hypertension in pregnancy or the postpartum period, given by mouth (swallowed, never sublingual/bitten), 10–20 mg with 20-minute re-checks, under a protocol with defined escalation. It is not an approved or appropriate agent for acute BP lowering in any non-pregnant adult — the FDA label explicitly says so — and sublingual administration is never appropriate in anyone.**

### 6d. Nifedipine ER — the legitimate chronic agent
**Label (Procardia XL):** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=8ebcb33c-f43b-4b36-9f94-9774b2a59e06
- Indicated for **hypertension** as well as vasospastic and chronic stable angina.
- "Therapy for either hypertension or angina should be initiated with **30 or 60 mg once daily**"; "titration should proceed over a **7–14 day** period"; **"titration to doses above 120 mg are not recommended."**
- **Swallow whole, do not bite or divide.** Plasma plateau ~6 h after the first dose.
- Not affected by renal impairment.
- The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline names **extended-release nifedipine** (with labetalol) as a preferred first-line agent for chronic hypertension in pregnancy.

## 7. HYDROCHLOROTHIAZIDE

**Class:** thiazide diuretic.
**Labels:** 12.5 mg capsules — https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=a3eb7294-f913-434b-896c-da028bfebc73 ; 25/50 mg tablets — https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=babb1b20-fe82-40ba-8e7a-0c56fe433b4a

**Dosing:**
- Capsule label: "The adult initial dose … is one capsule [12.5 mg] given once daily"; **"Total daily doses greater than 50 mg are not recommended."** In the elderly, "starting treatment with the lowest available dose of hydrochlorothiazide (12.5 mg) is therefore recommended. If further titration is required, 12.5 mg increments should be utilized."
- Tablet label: "The usual initial dose in adults is 25 mg daily … may be increased to 50 mg daily … **Doses above 50 mg are often associated with marked reductions in serum potassium.**"

### The onset/duration question — the card is quoting the diuretic curve
- **Tablet label (diuresis):** "After oral use **diuresis begins within 2 hours, peaks in about 4 hours and lasts about 6 to 12 hours**." ← this is exactly the card's "onset 2 h / duration 6–12 h."
- **Capsule label (pharmacologic/antihypertensive activity):** "Thiazides do not affect normal blood pressure. **Onset of action occurs within 2 hours of dosing, peak effect is observed at about 4 hours, and activity persists for up to 24 hours.**"
- So the card's duration understates the antihypertensive effect by half a day. Plasma t½ is **6–15 h** and it is dosed once daily precisely because activity persists ~24 h.
- **Time to full antihypertensive effect:** not stated in the label. The pivotal HCTZ monotherapy trial cited in the label ran **4 weeks**; mechanistically, the acute effect is volume-mediated while "with chronic administration, plasma volume returns toward normal, but peripheral vascular resistance is decreased" — the vascular phase is what takes **2–4 weeks**. Do not judge a thiazide by day 2.

**Dose/benefit:** "In published placebo-controlled trials comparing 12.5 mg of hydrochlorothiazide to 25 mg, **the 12.5 mg dose preserved most of the placebo-corrected blood pressure reduction seen with 25 mg**," and "clinically significant hypokalemia has been consistently less common in patients who received 12.5 mg … than in patients who received higher doses." Adverse events at 12.5 mg were "comparable to placebo"; the listed AE profile applies to **25 mg and greater**.

**Cautions:** contraindicated in anuria and sulfonamide hypersensitivity. Hypokalemia, hyponatremia, hypochloremic alkalosis, hypomagnesemia, hypercalcemia; hyperuricemia/gout; hyperglycemia; hyperlipidemia; **acute transient myopia and acute angle-closure glaucoma** (hours to weeks after initiation — stop the drug). Lithium toxicity. NSAIDs blunt the effect.
**Renal:** "Cumulative effects of the drug may develop in patients with impaired renal function… thiazides may precipitate azotemia." Plasma levels rise and t½ is prolonged in renal disease.

### The CKD / eGFR question
- **Traditional teaching (what the card's "CKD 1-3a/b" encodes):** thiazides lose natriuretic potency as GFR falls because less drug is secreted into the tubular lumen; switch to a loop diuretic below **eGFR ~30 mL/min/1.73 m²**.
- **CLICK (Agarwal, *NEJM* 2021;385:2507)** overturns this for chlorthalidone: 160 patients with **stage 4 CKD (eGFR 15–30)** and uncontrolled hypertension on a mean of 3.4 agents (60% on a loop diuretic), randomized to chlorthalidone 12.5 mg/d titrated q4 weeks to max 50 mg/d vs placebo. At 12 weeks: **24-hour ambulatory SBP −10.5 mm Hg vs placebo**; **UACR 50% lower**. Costs: hypokalemia, reversible eGFR decline, hyperuricemia, dizziness — worst in those **also on a loop diuretic**. https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2110730
- **KDIGO 2021** accordingly no longer advises avoiding thiazides in advanced CKD: chlorthalidone, metolazone and indapamide "appear to remain effective at GFR <30." Review: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9455225/ and https://academic.oup.com/ckj/article/16/1/41/6693719
- **Practical reading:** HCTZ itself remains weak at low eGFR (short half-life, no advanced-CKD trial). **Chlorthalidone**, not HCTZ, is the thiazide that works at eGFR 15–30 — and when added to a loop, check K+ and creatinine within 1–2 weeks.

## 8. ISOSORBIDE DINITRATE (and mononitrate)

**Class:** organic nitrate (predominantly venodilator; arterial at higher doses).
**ISDN label (Isordil Titradose):** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=8ddd2577-311a-4967-bb67-ad72e2b76f83

**Dosing (label, verbatim):** "The usual starting dose of Isordil Titradose is **5 mg to 20 mg two or three times daily**." "For maintenance therapy, **10 mg to 40 mg two or three times daily** is recommended."

**Effect timing (label, verbatim):**
- "**Anti-anginal activity is present about 1 hour after dosing.**"
- "Serum levels reach their maxima about an hour after ingestion."
- "Controlled trials of single oral doses … have demonstrated effective reductions in exercise-related angina for **up to 8 hours**."
- t½: ISDN ~1 h; **isosorbide-5-mononitrate metabolite ~5 h**; 2-mononitrate ~2 h. (The 5-mononitrate metabolite is what carries the effect — which is why the metabolite is marketed as its own drug.)

**Onset — your suspicion, checked:** the **1 hour** on the card is the label figure and is **correct for oral immediate-release ISDN**. The fast nitrate is **sublingual** ISDN (2.5–5 mg, onset ~2–5 min) and sublingual nitroglycerin — neither is on this card. So this one is CONFIRMED, not corrected.

**Nitrate-free interval (label, verbatim):** "**A daily dose-free interval of at least 14 hours is advisable to minimize tolerance.**" "Maintenance of continuous 24-hour plasma levels results in refractory tolerance. **Every dosing regimen … must provide a daily dose-free interval.**" Dose escalation does not overcome tolerance.
→ The card's "TID not q8h" is exactly right: e.g. **0800 / 1300 / 1800** leaves 14 h nitrate-free; 0800/1600/2400 leaves only 8 h and will produce tolerance within days.

**Isosorbide mononitrate:**
- **IR (tablets):** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=0d739bf3-0672-4504-9064-b39d6b200b47 — "The recommended regimen … is **20 mg twice daily, with the doses seven hours apart**" (so a **17-hour** nitrate-free gap). Start 5 mg only in very small patients, up to ≥10 mg by day 2–3. Antianginal efficacy begins **1 hour after the first dose** and lasts **at least 7 hours after the second dose**. Doses above 20 mg BID not adequately studied.
- **ER:** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=8cbb364a-bafe-454b-a53f-bd7cd69a5593 — **30 or 60 mg once daily in the morning on arising**; after several days may increase to **120 mg** once daily; rarely 240 mg. Do not chew or crush. Provides **at least 12 hours** of antianginal activity. Absolute bioavailability ~100% (no first-pass), peak 30–60 min for IR.
- **Note the card's shorthand "Mononitrate = long-acting" is only true of the ER form.** ISMN *immediate-release* is a twice-daily, 7-hours-apart drug.

**Contraindications:** PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil) and **riociguat**. "Severe hypotension, particularly with upright posture, may occur with even small doses." Headache is the most common adverse effect and is often dose-limiting. Also avoid in HCM with outflow obstruction, severe AS, and suspected RV infarct.

**The "ESRD" indication — rationale:** it is **not a labeled indication**; it is clinical convention with a sound pharmacologic basis. The ISMN IR label states: "**Dosage adjustments are not necessary for elderly patients or patients with altered hepatic or renal function**," and the ER label: "The elimination half-life of ISMN was not prolonged, and there was no drug accumulation in patients with chronic renal failure after multiple oral dosing." In dialysis-dependent patients, RAAS blockers are often limited by hyperkalemia, diuretics are useless, and interdialytic BP is volume- and vasoconstriction-driven — a non-renally-cleared vasodilator pair (**nitrate + hydralazine**) fills that gap and requires no dose adjustment. Recommend the card say "ESRD (no renal dose adjustment; pairs with hydralazine when RAASi/diuretics are not options)" rather than implying an indication.

**Hydralazine + nitrate in HFrEF (both drugs are on this card):**
- Fixed-dose combination label (ISDN 20 mg / hydralazine 37.5 mg): https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=8f54d5a3-169f-48dd-9250-2c176d2bd9ad
- "**One tablet three times a day titrated to a maximum tolerated dose up to two tablets three times a day**" (i.e. up to ISDN 120 mg + hydralazine 225 mg/day). May reduce to half a tablet TID for intolerable side effects, then titrate back up. Most common adverse effects: **headache and dizziness**.
- Indication: "treatment of heart failure as an adjunct to standard therapy **in self-identified black patients** to improve survival, prolong time to hospitalization for heart failure, and improve patient-reported functional status."
- **A-HeFT** (Taylor, *NEJM* 2004;351:2049): 1,050 patients with NYHA III–IV HFrEF; stopped early; mortality **6.2% vs 10.2%** (HR 0.57, 43% improvement in survival, p=0.01), **39% fewer HF hospitalizations**. https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa042934
- Note the internal tension worth flagging on the card: hydralazine is **contraindicated in CAD** per its own label, yet the hydralazine/nitrate combination is a class I HFrEF therapy — the nitrate offsets the reflex/ischemic problem, which is exactly why the two are never used apart in this setting.

## 9. METOPROLOL (tartrate and succinate ER)

**Class:** beta-1 selective blocker (selectivity is dose-dependent and lost at higher doses).
**Tartrate label:** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=0ca72c89-82c5-4b31-b515-1a8b8e82ec59
**Succinate ER label:** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=31d8610a-ab22-49e5-a762-6b08244ee93a

**Tartrate dosing (label):** hypertension — "The usual initial dosage is **100 mg daily in single or divided doses**. Adjust dosage at **weekly (or longer) intervals** … **In general, the maximum effect of any given dosage level will be apparent after 1 week of therapy.** The effective dosage range … is **100 mg to 450 mg per day**." Caveat in the label: "lower doses (especially 100 mg) may not maintain a full effect at the end of the 24-hour period. Larger or more frequent daily doses may be required."
Angina: 100 mg/day in two divided doses; range 100–400 mg/day.
Post-MI (label protocol): 5 mg IV q2 min × 3 doses, then **50 mg PO q6h starting 15 min after the last IV dose** for 48 h, then 100 mg BID.

**Succinate ER dosing (label):** hypertension — "**The usual initial dosage is 25 to 100 mg daily in a single dose**," increase at weekly or longer intervals, max effect of a dose apparent after 1 week, "**dosages above 400 mg per day have not been studied**."
Heart failure: **25 mg daily × 2 weeks** for NYHA II, **12.5 mg daily** for more severe HF; **double every 2 weeks** to max tolerated or **200 mg**.

### Conversions (both label-sourced — worth putting on the card)
- **Tartrate → succinate ER:** "For treatment of hypertension and angina, when switching from immediate-release metoprolol to metoprolol succinate extended-release tablets, **the same total daily dose … should be used**." → **1:1 on total daily dose.**
  Caveat: ER gives lower peaks and a flatter profile (peak plasma one-quarter to one-half that of IR; ~77% relative bioavailability at steady state) while delivering comparable 24-hour beta-1 blockade. To match the ER peak-to-trough ratio at 200–400 mg/day, IR would have to be dosed TID–QID.
- **IV → PO:** the tartrate label states it directly — "**Equivalent maximal beta-blocking effect is achieved with oral and intravenous doses in the ratio of approximately 2.5:1.**" → **PO dose ≈ 2.5 × IV dose** (5 mg IV ≈ 12.5 mg PO; 5 mg IV q6h ≈ 12.5 mg PO q6h). Oral bioavailability ~50% (saturable first-pass).

**Kinetics:** t½ **3–4 h** (tartrate; 7–9 h in CYP2D6 poor metabolizers); significant beta-blockade **within 1 hour** of an oral dose; IV maximum beta-blockade at ~20 min.
**Renal:** **no dose reduction needed in renal failure** — availability and half-life are unchanged. Not meaningfully dialyzed.
**Hepatic:** start low, titrate slowly.
**Cautions:** taper over 1–2 weeks (abrupt cessation → angina exacerbation, MI); "avoid abruptly discontinuing … in patients treated only for hypertension." CYP2D6 inhibitors (fluoxetine, paroxetine, bupropion, quinidine, propafenone) raise levels 2–5×, eroding beta-1 selectivity. Avoid initiating high-dose beta blockade before noncardiac surgery (bradycardia, hypotension, stroke, death). Masks hypoglycemia and hyperthyroid tachycardia. **Withdraw the beta blocker several days before stopping clonidine**, or it will worsen clonidine rebound.
**Preferred in:** HFrEF (succinate only — this is the salt with mortality data), post-MI, rate control in AF, angina, aortic dissection.

**Belongs on the card? YES — essential.** It is the most frequently used oral antihypertensive/rate-control agent on a medicine floor, and the two salts are not interchangeable in heart failure (succinate has the outcome data; tartrate does not). The IV:PO 2.5:1 ratio is used almost daily and is not on any current line of the card.

## 10. CARVEDILOL

**Class:** non-selective beta blocker with alpha-1 blockade.
**Label (Coreg):** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=07c619c6-4a0c-445e-94fa-664a54f68a39

**Dosing (label, verbatim):**
- **Hypertension:** "The recommended starting dose … is **6.25 mg twice daily**. If this dose is tolerated, using **standing** systolic pressure measured about 1 hour after dosing as a guide, the dose should be maintained for **7 to 14 days**, and then increased to **12.5 mg twice daily** … maintained for 7 to 14 days and can then be adjusted upward to **25 mg twice daily**. **The full antihypertensive effect of COREG is seen within 7 to 14 days. Total daily dose should not exceed 50 mg.**"
- **Heart failure:** "**3.125 mg twice daily for 2 weeks**. If tolerated … increased to 6.25, 12.5, and 25 mg twice daily over successive intervals of **at least 2 weeks**." Max 50 mg BID if >85 kg.
- **Post-MI LV dysfunction:** 6.25 mg BID, increase after 3–10 days to 12.5 then 25 mg BID.

**Food requirement:** "**COREG should be taken with food to slow the rate of absorption and reduce the incidence of orthostatic effects.**" This is a labeled instruction, not a preference — carvedilol given on an empty stomach produces first-dose orthostasis.

**The alpha-blockade advantage:** "Due to the α1-receptor blocking activity of carvedilol, **blood pressure is lowered more in the standing than in the supine position**." Net effect is vasodilation plus beta blockade, so carvedilol lowers BP more than metoprolol at equivalent beta blockade, and does not worsen insulin resistance — GEMINI showed no adverse effect on HbA1c (mean change +0.02%, 95% CI −0.06 to 0.10).

**Kinetics:** bioavailability 25–35%; t½ 7–10 h; CYP2D6 substrate.
**Cautions:** hypotension/syncope highest in the first 30 days (hypotension 9.7%, syncope 3.4% in mild-moderate HF trials; 15.1%/2.9% in COPERNICUS). Worsening HF or fluid retention during up-titration — increase the diuretic, hold the dose advance, do not abandon the drug. Bronchospastic disease: non-selective, so use with caution and lower the dose at any wheeze. Rare deterioration of renal function in HF with SBP <100, ischemic/diffuse vascular disease, or baseline renal insufficiency — reversible on withdrawal.
**Renal:** no renal dose adjustment specified; not dialyzable (high protein binding).

**Why preferred in HFrEF:** mortality benefit in US Carvedilol Program, COPERNICUS (NYHA IV, EF <25%), and CAPRICORN (post-MI EF ≤40%); **COMET** directly compared carvedilol (target 25 mg BID) with immediate-release metoprolol **tartrate** (target 50 mg BID) in 3,029 patients and favored carvedilol — which is why carvedilol and metoprolol **succinate** (not tartrate) are the guideline beta blockers in HFrEF. EF gain averaged +9 points vs +2 with placebo, dose-dependently.

**Belongs on the card? YES — essential.** It is the beta blocker of choice in HFrEF and in cirrhosis, it lowers BP more than metoprolol, and the "take with food" instruction prevents a common first-dose event.

## 11. CLONIDINE (oral tablets and transdermal patch)

**Class:** central alpha-2 agonist (reduces sympathetic outflow from the brainstem).
**Tablet label:** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=8171776a-dd14-4dcc-8860-3c27d8ea9504
**Patch label (Catapres-TTS):** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=39e1d35e-533c-4647-8649-8168a6e92dfa

**Oral dosing (label):** initial **0.1 mg twice daily** (morning and bedtime; lower in the elderly). "Further increments of **0.1 mg per day** may be made at **weekly intervals**." Usual therapeutic range **0.2–0.6 mg/day** in divided doses. "**2.4 mg is the maximum effective daily dose**," rarely used. Give the larger portion at bedtime to limit dry mouth and drowsiness.
**Oral effect timing (label, verbatim):** "Clonidine hydrochloride tablets act relatively rapidly. **The patient's blood pressure declines within 30 to 60 minutes after an oral dose, the maximum decrease occurring within 2 to 4 hours.**"
**Kinetics:** elimination t½ **12–16 h**, rising to **41 h in severe renal impairment**.
**Renal:** lower initial dose; monitor closely; **minimally removed by hemodialysis** — no supplemental dose needed.

**Patch dosing (label):** apply **once every 7 days** to a hairless area of intact upper outer arm or chest, rotating sites. TTS-1 / -2 / -3 deliver **0.1 / 0.2 / 0.3 mg per day**. Start with TTS-1; "if after **one or two weeks** the desired reduction in blood pressure is not achieved, increase the dosage by adding another … -1 or changing to a larger system." Above two TTS-3 there is no added efficacy.
**The 2–3 day lag (label, verbatim):** "**Therapeutic plasma clonidine levels are achieved 2 to 3 days after initial application**," and "When substituting Catapres-TTS for oral clonidine or for other antihypertensive drugs, physicians should be aware that **the antihypertensive effect … may not commence until 2–3 days after initial application. Therefore, gradual reduction of prior drug dosage is advised.**" On removal, levels persist ~8 h then decline over several days.
Practical corollary: a patch placed to cover a patient who is NPO or being taken off oral clonidine does **nothing for 48–72 hours**, and the oral drug must be overlapped, not stopped.

### Rebound hypertension (label, verbatim)
"Sudden cessation of clonidine treatment has, in some cases, resulted in symptoms such as nervousness, agitation, headache, and tremor accompanied or followed by a **rapid rise in blood pressure and elevated catecholamine concentrations**… The likelihood of such reactions … appears to be **greater after administration of higher doses or continuation of concomitant beta-blocker treatment**… **Rare instances of hypertensive encephalopathy, cerebrovascular accidents and death have been reported after clonidine withdrawal.** When discontinuing … reduce the dose **gradually over 2 to 4 days**."
"If therapy is to be discontinued in patients receiving a beta-blocker and clonidine concurrently, **the beta-blocker should be withdrawn several days before** the gradual discontinuation of clonidine." An excessive rebound can be reversed with oral clonidine or IV **phentolamine**.
The metoprolol label carries the mirror-image warning: "**Beta-blockers including metoprolol may exacerbate the rebound hypertension that can follow the withdrawal of clonidine.**"

### Why scheduled/PRN clonidine in hospital is a criticized practice
1. **It is pharmacologically the worst possible PRN drug.** BP falls within 30–60 min with a 2–4 h nadir, but t½ is 12–16 h (up to 41 h in CKD). Doses given "for SBP >180" therefore stack, and the patient becomes hypotensive and bradycardic hours later, often overnight.
2. **A PRN order creates a drug you cannot stop.** Once a patient has received clonidine repeatedly, abrupt discontinuation at discharge or on transfer risks rebound hypertension, encephalopathy, or stroke — a hazard entirely created by the PRN order.
3. **It masks the real problem.** Pain, anxiety, urinary retention, withdrawal, omitted home meds — the 2025 guideline's listed reversible contributors — go unaddressed.
4. **Sedation and delirium** in exactly the inpatient population most vulnerable to both.

**Sources criticizing PRN antihypertensives in hospital:**
- **2025 AHA/ACC guideline** — for SBP >180 or DBP >120 without acute target-organ damage, "advises against the intermittent use of intravenous or oral antihypertensive medications solely for the purpose of acute BP reduction." https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/HYP.0000000000000249 ; open-access summary: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12995957/
- **Mohandas et al., *Hypertension* 2021;78:516–524** — propensity-matched cohort, 42,380 admissions, 4,219 matched pairs. PRN antihypertensives were associated with **AKI OR 1.24 (1.09–1.42)**, **ischemic stroke OR 8.50 (1.96–36.79)**, **abrupt >25% SBP drop within 1 h OR 2.05 (1.56–2.71)**, **in-hospital mortality OR 2.36 (1.26–4.41)**, and LOS 4.7 vs 2.9 days. Conclusion: "routine use of as-needed antihypertensive medication should be discouraged." https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8266743/
- **Gaynor et al., *Ther Adv Cardiovasc Dis* 2018;12:7–15** — 250 patients, 573 PRN doses (91% oral hydralazine); **36% were given for SBP <180 and DBP <110**; 40.8% of patients were not continued on their home antihypertensives; 62.4% had no regimen intensification at discharge. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29265003/
- **Anderson et al., *JAMA Intern Med* 2019;179:1528–1536** — intensifying antihypertensives at discharge in older adults increased readmissions and serious adverse events without improving BP at 1 year. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31424475/

**Belongs on the card? YES — but as a hazard entry, not a treatment entry.** Clonidine's place on an internal medicine card is (a) recognize rebound hypertension in a patient whose home clonidine was held, (b) never write it PRN, (c) know the patch takes 2–3 days.

## 12. LOSARTAN (and valsartan)

**Class:** angiotensin II receptor blocker.
**Losartan label (Cozaar):** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=9949448f-c3b9-44ee-94ed-c1aca8c90f39
**Valsartan label (Diovan):** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=5ddba454-f3e6-43c2-a7a6-58365d297213

**Losartan dosing (label):** hypertension — "The usual starting dose … is **50 mg once daily**. The dosage can be increased to a maximum dose of **100 mg once daily**." **25 mg** start "for patients with possible intravascular depletion (e.g., on diuretic therapy)." **Hepatic impairment: 25 mg once daily.** No renal dose adjustment specified (contrast with lisinopril), but avoid aliskiren if GFR <60.
Nephropathy in type 2 diabetes: 50 mg daily → 100 mg. Stroke risk reduction in LVH: 50 mg daily + HCTZ 12.5 mg, → losartan 100 mg + HCTZ 25 mg.

**Losartan effect timing (label, verbatim):** "**The effect of losartan is substantially present within one week but in some studies the maximal effect occurred in 3–6 weeks.**" Peak effect **6 hours** post-dose; trough-to-peak ratio 50–95% systolic / 60–90% diastolic. "**There is no apparent rebound effect after abrupt withdrawal of losartan.**" No change in heart rate.
Note the label's own caution: twice-daily dosing at the same total daily dose gave "consistently larger trough responses" than once-daily — losartan is the ARB most likely to fade before 24 h.

**Valsartan dosing (label):** hypertension — **80 or 160 mg once daily** (higher start for larger reductions needed); range **80–320 mg/day**. "**The antihypertensive effect is substantially present within 2 weeks and maximal reduction is generally attained after 4 weeks.**" "Addition of a diuretic has a greater effect than dose increases beyond 80 mg." HF: 40 mg BID → 80 → 160 mg BID. Post-MI: 20 mg BID → 160 mg BID.

**Cautions (both):** boxed fetal toxicity — discontinue in pregnancy. Do not combine with an ACEi or aliskiren. Hyperkalemia (CKD, K supplements, K-sparing agents). Acute renal failure with bilateral renal artery stenosis. Hypotension in volume-depleted patients. NSAIDs blunt the effect and increase renal risk. Angioedema is far less common than with ACEi; a patient with ACEi angioedema may receive an ARB **beginning 6 weeks after the ACEi is stopped** (2025 guideline).

### Losartan's uricosuric effect
Losartan (the parent compound, not its EXP3174 metabolite) **inhibits URAT1** in the proximal tubule, reducing net urate reabsorption and lowering serum uric acid — an effect **not shared by other ARBs**. Peak uricosuria occurs 2–4 h after a dose.
- Head-to-head: losartan vs candesartan in hypertensive patients with diuretic-associated hyperuricemia — both lowered BP equally, **only losartan lowered uric acid**. *Am J Hypertens* 2006;19:208. https://academic.oup.com/ajh/article/19/2/208/128815
- Mechanism/URAT1: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.2217/pgs.15.52
- In LIFE, reduction in serum uric acid accounted for a portion of losartan's cardiovascular benefit over atenolol. *Kidney Int* 2004. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14871425/

**Practical value:** in a hypertensive patient with gout or on a thiazide, losartan is the ARB to pick. It does not replace urate-lowering therapy but it does not fight it.

**Belongs on the card? YES — essential.** The card says "can use ARB if ACEi intolerance" but names no drug or dose. Losartan 50 mg qd (25 mg if volume-depleted or hepatic) with the 3–6 week full-effect caveat and the gout pearl is worth one line.

## 13. CHLORTHALIDONE

**Class:** thiazide-**like** diuretic (sulfonamide, not a benzothiadiazine).
**Label:** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=2de92cea-8f03-4af0-9d91-d4578510cc53

**Dosing (label):** hypertension — "Therapy, in most patients, should be initiated with a single daily dose of **25 mg**. If the response is insufficient … increase to **50 mg** … If additional control is required … **100 mg once daily** or add a second agent. **Dosage above 100 mg daily usually does not increase effectiveness.** Increases in serum uric acid and decreases in serum potassium are dose-related over the 25 to 100 mg/day range."
*(The 2017 ACC/AHA guideline's usual range is lower — 12.5–25 mg once daily — and 12.5 mg is the practical start; CLICK also started at 12.5 mg.)*

**Potency and duration vs HCTZ (label):** "an oral diuretic with **prolonged action (48–72 hours)**"; "The diuretic effect of the drug occurs in approximately **2.6 hours** and continues for up to **72 hours**"; mean t½ **40 hours** after 50–200 mg (elimination t½ 53 h after 50 mg, 60 h after 100 mg). Compare HCTZ: diuresis 6–12 h, activity ~24 h, t½ 6–15 h.
**Milligram potency:** chlorthalidone is **~1.5–2× more potent than HCTZ mg-for-mg**, with a substantially longer half-life — so 12.5–25 mg chlorthalidone ≈ 25–50 mg HCTZ, with better overnight and 24-hour coverage.

**Cautions:** same class effects as HCTZ but more pronounced — **greater potassium lowering** than HCTZ, more hyponatremia, more hyperuricemia. "Should be used with caution in severe renal disease … may precipitate azotemia."

### Guideline position: 2017 vs 2025 — this changed
- **2017 ACC/AHA:** initial therapy should be a "thiazide or thiazide-type diuretic (**preferably chlorthalidone**)" — based on its longer half-life and the outcome data (ALLHAT, SHEP). https://www.ccjm.org/content/85/10/771
- **2025 AHA/ACC: that preference has been removed.** The guideline places "less emphasis on chlorthalidone over traditional thiazides," informed by the **Diuretic Comparison Project (DCP)** — a pragmatic, EHR-embedded VA randomized trial (n=13,523, mean age 73) in which patients already on HCTZ 25 mg/day either continued it or switched to chlorthalidone, with **no difference in cardiovascular events** and comparable follow-up BP. DCP's limitations (low event rate, pragmatic outcome ascertainment, questions about the chlorthalidone dosing strategy) are acknowledged. The guideline's 2025 comment table simply notes that "chlorthalidone has a longer half-life and is more potent than hydrochlorothiazide on a mg-to-mg basis," leaving the choice to the clinician.
  Review with the guideline's reasoning: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12911370/
- **Where chlorthalidone still clearly wins:** advanced CKD (CLICK, eGFR 15–30, SBP −10.5 mm Hg) and resistant hypertension, where the longer half-life and greater potency matter. https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2110730

**Belongs on the card? YES — essential, and it should replace or sit beside HCTZ.** The single most useful line is: *chlorthalidone 12.5–25 mg qd; ~1.5–2× HCTZ potency, 40–60 h half-life, 48–72 h action; the thiazide that still works at eGFR 15–30 (CLICK); costs more K+.*

## 14. DILTIAZEM and VERAPAMIL (oral)

**Class:** non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (vasodilation **plus** negative chronotropy/inotropy and AV nodal slowing).

### Diltiazem
- **Immediate-release tablets (Cardizem):** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=f3e7ecef-f360-4987-a4f5-933214130ab2 — **"CARDIZEM is indicated for the management of chronic stable angina and angina due to coronary artery spasm." Hypertension is not an indication for the IR tablet.** Kinetics: detectable plasma levels within **30–60 min**, peak plasma **2–4 h**; usual 30 mg QID up to 360 mg/day.
- **Extended-release (Cardizem LA):** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=3e180dc7-c871-4efc-8755-cae882976c8d — indicated for **hypertension**. "Initiate dosing at **180 to 240 mg once daily** … Titrate according to blood pressure to a **maximum of 540 mg daily**. **Maximum antihypertensive effect is usually observed by 14 days of chronic therapy.**" Angina: 180 mg qd, max 360 mg. Most common AEs in hypertension: lower-limb edema, sinus congestion, rash.

### Verapamil
- **Extended-release tablets:** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=ed182ebe-8299-4680-9dea-ef79d30db683 — indicated for hypertension. "**Initiate therapy with 180 mg** … given in the morning. **Lower initial doses of 120 mg a day** may be warranted in patients who may have an increased response (e.g., the elderly or small people). Upward titration should be based on … efficacy and safety **evaluated weekly**… **The antihypertensive effects … are evident within the first week of therapy.**" Titration: 240 mg qAM → 180 BID or 240 qAM + 120 qPM → 240 q12h. Max recommended human daily dose **480 mg/day**. **Administer with food.**
- "Verapamil exerts antihypertensive effects by decreasing systemic vascular resistance, **usually without orthostatic decreases in blood pressure or reflex tachycardia**; bradycardia (rate <50) is uncommon (1.4%)."
- **Constipation 7.3%** — the dose-limiting effect, and a real problem in immobile inpatients.

**Cautions (both):** avoid in **HFrEF** (negative inotropes), sick sinus syndrome or 2nd/3rd-degree AV block without a pacemaker, and with beta blockers — "there have been reports of excessive bradycardia and AV block, including complete heart block, when the combination has been used for the treatment of hypertension. For hypertensive patients, **the risks of combined therapy may outweigh the potential benefits**." Verapamil reduces metoprolol and propranolol clearance. Both are CYP3A4 inhibitors/substrates (statin, tacrolimus, apixaban interactions); verapamil raises digoxin levels.

**Where they fit inpatient:** the patient who needs **both** BP and rate control — AF with RVR plus hypertension, or hypertension with angina and a contraindication to beta blockade (e.g. severe asthma) — and specifically **not** the patient with reduced EF. IV diltiazem → oral transition is the common inpatient sequence; note that for **hypertension** the labeled oral form is the ER product (180–240 mg qd), not IR TID/QID dosing.

**Belongs on the card? YES for diltiazem (moderately essential).** The IV-diltiazem-to-oral conversion and the "ER for BP, IR only for angina/rate" distinction are frequently gotten wrong. Verapamil is lower yield on a medicine card.

## 15. SPIRONOLACTONE

**Class:** mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (non-selective; also blocks androgen and progesterone receptors).
**Label (Aldactone):** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=0fed2822-3a03-4b64-9857-c682fcd462bc

**Dosing (label, verbatim):**
- **Essential hypertension:** "The recommended initial daily dose is **25 to 100 mg** … in either single or divided doses. **Dosage can be titrated at two-week intervals. Doses greater than 100 mg/day generally do not provide additional reductions in blood pressure.**"
- **Heart failure:** with **K ≤5.0 and eGFR >50** → **25 mg once daily**; may increase to 50 mg once daily. Hyperkalemia at 25 mg daily → reduce to **25 mg every other day**. **eGFR 30–50 → consider starting at 25 mg every other day** because of hyperkalemia risk.
- Edema/cirrhosis: 100 mg/day (range 25–200), initiate in hospital and titrate slowly; give ≥5 days as sole diuretic before increasing.

**Contraindications (label):** **hyperkalemia**, **Addison's disease**, concomitant **eplerenone**.
**Warnings:** "Monitor serum potassium **within one week of initiation** and regularly thereafter." Risk is greater in renal impairment. **Gynecomastia is the most common adverse reaction** (dose- and duration-related; eplerenone or finerenone if intolerable). Also: hyperkalemic metabolic acidosis with cholestyramine; NSAIDs, ACEi/ARB, trimethoprim, heparin and K supplements all compound hyperkalemia.

**Role in resistant hypertension — PATHWAY-2:** randomized, double-blind, crossover trial; patients with resistant hypertension (uncontrolled on an ACEi/ARB + CCB + thiazide at maximal tolerated doses) received 12-week rotations of **spironolactone 25–50 mg**, **bisoprolol 5–10 mg**, **doxazosin 4–8 mg**, and placebo. Spironolactone was **clearly superior to placebo and to both active comparators** in home systolic BP, and its benefit was largest in patients with the lowest plasma renin — supporting sodium retention as the dominant mechanism of resistant hypertension. *Lancet* 2015;386:2059. https://www.thelancet.com/article/S0140-6736(15)00257-3/fulltext
The **2025 AHA/ACC guideline** carries this forward: after an ACEi/ARB + CCB + thiazide-type diuretic, an MRA is the recommended fourth agent, and it now recommends screening **all** patients with stage 2 or resistant hypertension for **primary aldosteronism regardless of whether hypokalemia is present** (withhold MRAs ≥4 weeks before aldosterone testing).

**Hyperkalemia risk inpatient — the specific traps:** starting spironolactone in a patient already on an ACEi/ARB, on trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, on prophylactic heparin, or with an AKI in evolution; and continuing it through a period of poor oral intake or contrast exposure. Check K+ and creatinine within **one week** (sooner if eGFR 30–50 or K+ ≥4.5), and again after any renal insult.

**Belongs on the card? YES — essential.** It is the evidence-based fourth agent for resistant hypertension (which is much of what floor "difficult BP" actually is), it is a HFrEF mortality drug, and the hyperkalemia monitoring rule is concrete.

## 16. MINOXIDIL (oral)

**Class:** direct arteriolar vasodilator (ATP-sensitive potassium channel opener). Truly refractory hypertension only.
**Label:** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=0b4fc036-9497-442b-b629-c4b386932789

**Boxed warning (verbatim):** "Minoxidil tablets contain the powerful antihypertensive agent, minoxidil, which may produce serious adverse effects. **It can cause pericardial effusion, occasionally progressing to tamponade, and angina pectoris may be exacerbated.** Minoxidil should be reserved for hypertensive patients who **do not respond adequately to maximum therapeutic doses of a diuretic and two other antihypertensive agents**. Minoxidil must be administered under close supervision, **usually concomitantly with therapeutic doses of a beta-adrenergic blocking agent to prevent tachycardia** and increased myocardial workload. **It must also usually be given with a diuretic, frequently one acting in the ascending limb of the loop of Henle, to prevent serious fluid accumulation.**"

**Dosing (label):** initial **5 mg once daily**; increase to 10, 20, then 40 mg in single or divided doses; effective range usually **10–40 mg/day**; **maximum 100 mg/day**. "Intervals between dosage adjustments normally should be **at least 3 days** since the full response to a given dose is not obtained for at least that amount of time."

**Effect timing (label, verbatim):** "After an effective single oral dose, **blood pressure usually starts to decline within one-half hour, reaches a minimum between 2 and 3 hours** and recovers at an arithmetically linear rate of about 30%/day. **The total duration of effect is approximately 75 hours.**"
"**Maximum effect is achieved on 10 mg/day within 7 days, on 20 mg/day within 5 days, and on 40 mg/day within 3 days**" — the higher the dose, the faster the plateau.
Plasma t½ **4.2 hours** (note the enormous disconnect between plasma t½ and the 75-hour effect). Renal clearance corresponds to GFR; removable by hemodialysis.

**The two mandatory co-drugs:**
1. **Loop diuretic** — "If minoxidil is used without a diuretic, **retention of several hundred milliequivalents of salt and corresponding volumes of water can occur within a few days**, leading to increased plasma and interstitial fluid volume and local or generalized edema." A **high-ceiling (loop) diuretic is almost always required**; reversible edema still develops in ~10% of non-dialysis patients so treated. Temporary edema in 7% of patients not edematous at baseline. Refractory retention may require holding minoxidil for 1–2 days then resuming with vigorous diuresis.
2. **Beta blocker (or other sympathetic suppressant)** — "Minoxidil increases the heart rate. **Angina may worsen or appear for the first time** during minoxidil treatment… The increase in rate and the occurrence of angina generally can be prevented by the concomitant administration of a beta-adrenergic blocking drug… **Round-the-clock effectiveness of the sympathetic suppressant should be ensured.**"

**Pericardial effusion:** "**Pericardial effusion, occasionally with tamponade, has been observed in about 3% of treated patients not on dialysis**, especially those with inadequate or compromised renal function." Observe for any suggestion of pericardial disease; echocardiography if suspicion arises; may need more diuresis, dialysis, pericardiocentesis, or surgery; consider withdrawal if the effusion persists.
Also: hypertrichosis (near-universal, cosmetically limiting), ECG T-wave changes.

**Belongs on the card? NO — not essential for a general medicine floor card,** with one exception: if the card is used at a center with a nephrology/resistant-hypertension population, it is worth **one line** as a recognition item — "if a patient arrives on minoxidil, confirm they are also on a loop diuretic and a beta blocker, and have a low threshold for echo (pericardial effusion ~3%)." The dosing detail is not floor-level.

## 17. METHYLDOPA

**Class:** central alpha-2 agonist (prodrug → alpha-methylnorepinephrine).
**Label:** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=9e22e1d8-04ec-4b67-92ea-b09c706f2703

**Dosing (label, verbatim):** "The usual starting dosage … is **250 mg two to three times a day in the first 48 hours**. The daily dosage then may be increased or decreased, preferably at intervals of **not less than 2 days** … **To minimize the sedation, start dosage increases in the evening.**" Maintenance **500 mg to 2 g/day in two to four doses**; **maximum recommended 3 g/day**. If combined with a non-thiazide antihypertensive, limit the initial methyldopa dose to 500 mg/day in divided doses.

**Effect timing (label, verbatim):** "**The maximum decrease in blood pressure occurs four to six hours after oral dosage.** Once an effective dosage level is attained, **a smooth blood pressure response occurs in most patients in 12 to 24 hours.** After withdrawal, blood pressure usually returns to pretreatment levels within **24 to 48 hours**" — and this is "not complicated by an overshoot of blood pressure" (i.e. **no rebound**, unlike clonidine).
Tolerance may develop between the second and third month; add or increase a thiazide.

**Cautions:** contraindicated in **active hepatic disease** (acute hepatitis, active cirrhosis), prior methyldopa-associated liver disease, and with **MAO inhibitors**.
- **Positive direct Coombs test in 10–20%** of patients on prolonged therapy, usually between 6 and 12 months; lowest incidence at ≤1 g/day. Rarely progresses to **Coombs-positive hemolytic anemia**, which can be fatal — check CBC at baseline and periodically; direct Coombs at baseline, 6 and 12 months. The Coombs may stay positive for weeks to months after stopping, and a positive **indirect** Coombs complicates crossmatching.
- **Hepatotoxicity:** fever in the first 3 weeks with eosinophilia/LFT abnormalities; jaundice usually within the first 2–3 months; **rarely fatal hepatic necrosis**. Check LFTs during the first 6–12 weeks and with any unexplained fever. Never rechallenge.
- Sedation, especially on initiation and with each dose increase; orthostatic hypotension; drug fever; edema/weight gain; bradycardia; may aggravate angina.
**Renal:** "largely excreted by the kidney and patients with impaired renal function may respond to smaller doses."

**Why it is now second-line in pregnancy:** methyldopa's long safety record (decades of fetal follow-up) kept it first-line historically, but it is **weak and slow** — 4–6 h to peak effect per dose, 12–24 h to a smooth response, up to 4 doses a day, with sedation and depression that pregnant patients tolerate poorly, plus the Coombs/hepatitis monitoring burden. **Labetalol** and **extended-release nifedipine** achieve control faster with better tolerability and equivalent fetal safety, and the **2025 AHA/ACC guideline names labetalol and extended-release nifedipine as the preferred first-line agents** in pregnancy. Methyldopa remains an option when beta blockade and CCBs are contraindicated or not tolerated.
(ACEi, ARBs, direct renin inhibitors, nitroprusside, MRAs and atenolol are contraindicated in pregnancy.)

**Belongs on the card? NO for a general medicine card; YES if the card covers obstetric medicine.** One line at most: "methyldopa 250 mg BID–TID, now second-line in pregnancy behind labetalol and nifedipine ER; slow (12–24 h to a smooth response), sedating, Coombs-positive in 10–20%, hepatotoxic."

## 18. PRAZOSIN and DOXAZOSIN

**Class:** selective alpha-1 adrenergic antagonists.
**Prazosin label:** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=4f55f4db-dab3-4e50-9f71-5595fd3ccc12
**Doxazosin label (Cardura):** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=eefc47bc-c10f-49bb-8ef7-5999f798ab39

**Prazosin dosing (label):** **1 mg two or three times a day** initially. "Dosage may be slowly increased to a total daily dose of **20 mg** given in divided doses. The therapeutic dosages most commonly employed have ranged from **6 mg to 15 mg daily** in divided doses. Doses higher than 20 mg usually do not increase efficacy," though a few patients benefit up to 40 mg/day. Some can be maintained BID after titration. Peak plasma ~3 h; t½ **2–3 h**.

**Doxazosin dosing (label):** hypertension — "The initial dosage … is **1 mg given once daily**. Daily dosage may be **doubled** up to **16 mg once daily**, as needed." BPH: 1 mg daily, titrate at 1–2 week intervals up to 8 mg. Peak plasma **2–3 h**; food reduces Cmax 18% (not clinically significant).

### First-dose syncope (prazosin label, verbatim)
"As with all alpha-blockers, prazosin hydrochloride may cause **syncope with sudden loss of consciousness**… **Syncopal episodes have usually occurred within 30 to 90 minutes of the initial dose of the drug**; occasionally … in association with rapid dosage increases or the introduction of another antihypertensive drug… **The incidence of syncopal episodes is approximately 1% in patients given an initial dose of 2 mg or greater.** … syncopal episodes can be minimized by **limiting the initial dose of the drug to 1 mg**, by subsequently increasing the dosage slowly, and by introducing any additional antihypertensive drugs … with caution."
"Hypotension may develop in patients … who are also receiving a beta-blocker."
Doxazosin label: "Postural hypotension with or without symptoms … may develop **within a few hours** following administration … there is a potential for syncope, especially after the initial dose or after an increase in dosage strength." Postural hypotension 1.8%, syncope 0.1% in hypertensive trials; both dose-related.
**Practical rule:** first dose at bedtime, 1 mg, patient warned not to stand quickly; do not start on the same day another antihypertensive is added.

### The ALLHAT doxazosin arm
ALLHAT randomized high-risk hypertensives ≥55 to chlorthalidone, amlodipine, lisinopril or doxazosin. In **January 2000** the data review committee recommended **terminating the doxazosin arm early**. Versus chlorthalidone, doxazosin produced **no difference in the primary CHD endpoint** but a significantly higher rate of **combined CVD (RR 1.25; 95% CI 1.17–1.33)** and a **doubling of heart failure (RR 2.04; 95% CI 1.79–2.32)**. *JAMA* 2000;283:1967–1975. Operational report: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11165421/ ; HF event validation confirming the finding: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12459039/
**Consequence:** alpha-1 blockers have not been first- or second-line antihypertensives since. They are add-on agents, and their main legitimate use is the hypertensive man with symptomatic BPH, or pheochromocytoma (phenoxybenzamine/doxazosin preoperatively). PATHWAY-2 also found doxazosin inferior to spironolactone as a fourth agent.

**Belongs on the card? NO — not as an antihypertensive.** Worth at most a half-line caution: "alpha-1 blockers (doxazosin, prazosin, terazosin) are not antihypertensives — ALLHAT stopped the doxazosin arm for doubled heart failure; if the patient is on one it is usually for BPH, and the first dose causes syncope." The more clinically relevant inpatient fact is that these drugs are frequently on the home list for BPH or PTSD nightmares (prazosin) and contribute to inpatient orthostasis and falls.

## 19. FUROSEMIDE and TORSEMIDE (oral) — loops as antihypertensives

**Class:** loop diuretics (Na-K-2Cl inhibition at the thick ascending limb).
**Furosemide label (Lasix):** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=2c9b4d8f-0770-482d-a9e6-9c616a440b1a
**Torsemide label (Demadex):** https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=aff713e8-2cb6-4e49-94a2-c55c5491c822

### Furosemide
- **Hypertension dosing (label):** "**The usual initial dose … for hypertension is 80 mg, usually divided into 40 mg twice a day.** Dosage should then be adjusted according to response. If response is not satisfactory, add other antihypertensive agents."
- Label caveat worth quoting: "**Hypertensive patients who cannot be adequately controlled with thiazides will probably also not be adequately controlled with LASIX alone.**"
- Edema dosing: 20–80 mg as a single dose; may repeat or increase by 20–40 mg at 6–8 h intervals; up to 600 mg/day in severe edema.
- **Kinetics:** "The onset of diuresis following oral administration is **within 1 hour**. The peak effect occurs within the **first or second hour**. The duration of diuretic effect is **6 to 8 hours**." Terminal t½ ~**2 hours**. **Oral bioavailability only ~64%** of IV (and highly variable, worse with gut edema) — this is why the 1:2 IV:PO conversion exists and why oral furosemide fails in decompensated HF.
- Boxed warning: profound diuresis with water and electrolyte depletion if given in excessive amounts.

### Torsemide
- **Hypertension dosing (label):** "In patients with essential hypertension, DEMADEX … once a day at doses of **5 mg to 10 mg**. **The antihypertensive effect is near maximal after 4 to 6 weeks of treatment**, but it may continue to increase" thereafter.
- **Kinetics:** **bioavailability ~80%** and reliable (food delays but does not reduce absorption); peak plasma **within 1 hour**; **onset of diuresis within 1 hour**, duration **6 to 8 hours**. Longer and more predictable than furosemide.

### The loop-vs-thiazide question by eGFR
- **Historic rule:** switch from thiazide to loop at **eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m²**, because thiazide delivery to the tubular lumen falls with GFR.
- **What has changed:** **CLICK** (chlorthalidone 12.5→50 mg in stage 4 CKD, eGFR 15–30) achieved a **10.5 mm Hg** placebo-subtracted 24-hour ambulatory SBP reduction and a **50% fall in albuminuria** at 12 weeks — so thiazide-*like* agents demonstrably work below eGFR 30. **KDIGO 2021** now states that chlorthalidone, metolazone and indapamide "appear to remain effective at GFR <30" and no longer endorses avoiding thiazides in advanced CKD. https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2110730 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9455225/
- **Practical synthesis for a floor card:**
  - **Volume overload is the indication for a loop**, at any eGFR. Loops are better natriuretics; thiazides are better antihypertensives per mmHg.
  - **A loop alone is a poor antihypertensive** — 6–8 h of action means 16 hours a day of rebound sodium avidity; if used for BP it must be dosed **at least twice daily** (the furosemide label's 40 mg BID), and torsemide's ~80% bioavailability and longer action make it the better loop for BP.
  - **Loop + thiazide (sequential nephron blockade)** is the combination that works in CKD/resistant hypertension — and is also the combination with the highest hypokalemia and pre-renal AKI risk (this was the signal in CLICK). Check K+ and creatinine within 1–2 weeks.
  - In dialysis patients with no residual urine output, diuretics do nothing for BP; the answer is dry weight, and then non-renally-cleared vasodilators (nitrate + hydralazine, amlodipine).
- **Common floor error to flag:** substituting a loop for a thiazide in a hypertensive CKD patient who is euvolemic. That reliably worsens BP control.

**Belongs on the card? YES — moderately essential**, framed as the above rule rather than as an antihypertensive dosing entry: "loops treat volume, not blood pressure; if used for BP, dose BID (furosemide 40 mg BID) or use torsemide 5–10 mg qd; thiazide-like agents still work at eGFR 15–30 (CLICK)."
