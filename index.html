Picking a pair

Combination beats escalation. Doubling one drug buys about 1.5 mmHg; adding a second from a different class buys roughly six. The 2025 guideline recommends starting with two drugs in stage 2 hypertension and in higher-risk stage 1.

The principle

Pair agents that block different systems. Diuretics and calcium blockers both activate the renin–angiotensin system, which is exactly why adding an ACE inhibitor or ARB on top of either is more than additive — the second drug blocks the escape route the first one opened. Two drugs from the same system fight over one mechanism and add adverse effects without adding much effect.

The three first-line pairs

  • ACEi or ARB + dihydropyridine CCB — the strongest outcome evidence
  • ACEi or ARB + thiazide-type diuretic
  • Dihydropyridine CCB + thiazide-type diuretic — when a RAS blocker is not tolerated or is contraindicated

In ACCOMPLISH (11,506 high-risk patients), benazepril + amlodipine cut cardiovascular events 20% versus benazepril + hydrochlorothiazide, at the same blood pressure. Where both are reasonable, the calcium blocker is the better-evidenced partner.

Pairs that fix each other

  • ACEi/ARB + dihydropyridine — the RAS blocker blunts ankle oedema. Reach for this rather than a diuretic, which does not treat that oedema.
  • ACEi/ARB + thiazide — opposite potassium effects, so they cancel out.
  • Losartan + thiazide — losartan is mildly uricosuric and offsets thiazide-induced hyperuricaemia. Useful when gout coexists.
  • Thiazide + potassium-sparing agent — the guideline answer to thiazide-induced hypokalaemia.

Never, or almost never

  • ACEi + ARB — more hyperkalaemia and acute kidney injury, no outcome benefit. After ACEi angioedema you may start an ARB, but only 6 weeks later.
  • Non-dihydropyridine CCB + beta blocker — additive bradycardia and heart block.
  • Two potassium-sparing agents — hyperkalaemia.
  • Verapamil or diltiazem in HFrEF — negative inotropes.

Where these numbers come from

Every figure traces to Wang N, Salam A, Pant R, et al. "Blood pressure-lowering efficacy of antihypertensive drugs and their combinations." Lancet 2025;406:915–25 — a meta-analysis of 484 randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials in 104,176 participants, mean baseline 154/100 mmHg, mean follow-up 8.6 weeks.

The paper's public calculator at bpmodel.org answers one regimen at a time. This page holds the whole grid at once: each of the 53 drugs was queried at half, standard, and double standard dose, and the model's own baseline-BP sweep was recorded for each — 159 drug–dose cells in all.

The single-drug equation

From the paper's supplementary appendix (p. 14), efficacy for drug n at the reference baseline is log-linear in dose:

τb,n = βn · log(δn / δs,n) + αn ← α = effect at standard dose, β = gain per log dose ratio

and is then shifted linearly to the patient's actual baseline γ:

τγ,n = τb,n + (γ − 154) · εn ← ε = mmHg of extra effect per mmHg of higher baseline

Across all monotherapies the average is 8.7 mmHg systolic at standard dose, with each doubling of dose buying only about 1.5 mmHg more — the flattest part of the whole model, and the reason the paper argues for combining drugs rather than escalating one.

The combination equation

Combinations are not simply additive. Drugs are applied in sequence, each one seeing a baseline already lowered by those before it, so each successive agent earns less. Because that makes the answer depend on the order, the model averages over every ordering:

τγ,1 = τb,1 + (γ − 154) · ε1
τγ,2 = τb,2 + (γ − τγ,1 − 154) · ε2
…
τγ,P = (1 / n!) · Σ over all n! orderings ← the reported combination effect

How closely this page reproduces the original

Per-drug α, β and ε were recovered by least squares from the calculator's own outputs, then the permutation model was re-implemented here and checked against the live calculator twice: an initial 44-query run over in-range doses, and a later randomised audit of 40 more queries that deliberately included doses outside the fitted range and baselines from 130 to 180.

Read that as: inside the half-to-double standard dose range the model was fitted on, this page reproduces the source calculator to within rounding. Push a dose outside that range and the error roughly doubles, with a worst observed case of 1.8 mmHg — still small against the width of the prediction intervals, but no longer negligible. Ninety per cent of all audited queries landed within 1 mmHg of the calculator, and there is no systematic bias in either direction.

One honest limitation. The source calculator refuses to compute doses far above the trial standard dose — every query at ramipril 20 mg, captopril 300 mg, fosinopril 80 mg and nebivolol 40 mg came back empty. Those four rows are real prescribable doses and are shown, marked beyond model range, but their numbers are a pure extrapolation of the published equation that cannot be checked against the original. Treat them as a rough guide to magnitude, not as a validated estimate.

What the model does not tell you

  • Population means, not personal predictions. Individual response varies widely around these averages; the 95% prediction intervals shown are for the mean trial effect, not for a patient.
  • Short-term surrogate. Follow-up averaged 8.6 weeks, and the outcome is clinic blood pressure — not events, mortality, or ambulatory control.
  • Efficacy only. No adverse effects, adherence, monitoring burden, pregnancy safety, renal dosing, or cost enters any number here.
  • Thin data at the extremes. Drugs studied in a handful of small trials produce unstable point estimates that can top the ranking on noise alone.
  • Trial populations. Mean age 54, mean baseline 154/100 — older, frailer, or lower-baseline patients are extrapolation.

Doses, cost, and adverse effects

Three layers sit on top of the efficacy model, each from its own source.

Doses. Every strength shown is one that actually exists on a US shelf, so the ladder reflects prescribing steps rather than arithmetic multiples of the trial "standard dose". Where a real dose falls outside the half-to-double range the model was fitted on, the row is marked extrapolated.

Cost. This is an acquisition-cost benchmark, not what a patient pays: it ignores insurance, dispensing fees, and discount-card pricing. It is the right tool for "is this drug pennies or is it real money", which for these agents is usually the only question — most are under $5 a month, and the handful above $100 are worth knowing about. Two deliberate choices make the figures slightly conservative: the price is the median across manufacturers rather than the cheapest one, and the regimen is built from whole tablets rather than splitting a larger strength, which is usually cheaper but is not always what gets dispensed. Where a drug has both an immediate- and an extended-release form, the one priced is the form used for hypertension — so metoprolol is costed as succinate ER once daily, and the twice-daily tartrate is roughly a third of that.

Adverse effects. The column lists what you would counsel or watch for, not everything on the label. A few entries deliberately correct common teaching: the ACE inhibitor cough rate is the pooled ~11%, not the label's understated figure; urate-lowering is specific to losartan and is not an ARB class effect; beta blockers carry the abrupt-withdrawal warning as an ordinary warning, not a boxed one; and eplerenone's reputation for causing less gynecomastia than spironolactone is not supported by its own label, which reports no rate and makes no comparison.

Availability, and what this list is not

The universe here is the Wang trial set, not the US formulary. These 53 drugs are the ones the meta-analysis had placebo-controlled data for. Several agents you will reach for often are simply absent because the model has no coefficient for them — most notably labetalol (the default in pregnancy and in hypertensive urgency), triamterene/hydrochlorothiazide, torsemide, bumetanide, and metolazone. Their absence says nothing about how useful they are.

Of the 53, fourteen are marked non-US — never marketed in the United States or since withdrawn: barnidipine, lercanidipine, manidipine, nitrendipine, cilazapril, delapril, spirapril, fimasartan, bendroflumethiazide (listed here under its older name bendrofluazide), cyclopenthiazide, celiprolol, oxprenolol, eprosartan, and penbutolol.

Two more are US-marketed but hidden by default because both lost their once-daily formulation, leaving twice- and three-times-daily dosing that makes them impractical for chronic hypertension: isradipine and nicardipine. Quinapril is included but carries a supply warning — it has been in an FDA-listed shortage since 2023 with effectively one supplier, and has no entry at all in the current CMS pricing file, which is why its cost column is empty. It is a focusing convenience, not a formulary check — confirm against your own.
